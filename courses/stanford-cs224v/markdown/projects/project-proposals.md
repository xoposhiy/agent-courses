## Infrastructure for your Project![][image1] {#infrastructure-for-your-project}

This year, we are making available **Agentic OS (AOS)**, a new architecture on which you could create your own agents. You are welcome to use any tools you like. However, your projects must be using state-of-the-art tools and concepts, and cannot be built with vanilla LLMs by simply prompting them with the problem statement. 

AOS is an open, accountable platform for agents with a layered architecture that includes a **semantic file system** for retrieving and curating persistent knowledge and artifacts; a **harness** with experimental components to improve **reasoning, context management, conversation management, and decision making**; **team workflow support** for shared workspaces and collaboration. Together, these components provide a common substrate for building and coordinating advanced agent systems.

As an open-source platform, AOS promotes openness and broad technological progress while reducing dependence on the largest frontier models. Its shared libraries, research components, and systems abstractions make smaller and open-weight models more effective, enabling more cost-effective and environmentally sustainable deployments. AOS is designed to be **model-agnostic**, allowing organizations to use proprietary, open-weight, or domain-specific models without vendor lock-in. It also supports **data sovereignty** by enabling organizations to deploy, audit, extend, and govern the platform within their own environments, preserving control over sensitive data, model choices, policies, and operational constraints.

AOS has these notable components in the semantic file system: 

Ingestion: 

1. **CHURRO: A Visual-Language Model for historical prints and manuscripts:** Existing vision-language models (VLMs) are not equipped to read the diverse languages and scripts, irregular layouts, and frequent degradation found in historical materials. We collected the largest annotated historical dataset spanning 46 language clusters and 22 centuries, and trained a 7B open-weight VLM that is more accurate than the best commercial model at 0.6% of the cost.

Semantic Retrieval:

1. **SUQL (Structure and Unstructured Query Language):** a query language that composes information from databases and textual corpora.

2. **SLIDERS**:  Not only are long-context LLMs expensive to train and use, they are not accurate for long documents, and they are never long enough for there are many corpora with many documents. SLIDERS can handle sets of documents of arbitrary size by structuring long documents chunk by chunk. 

3. **SatIR**: a fast, high-precision, high-recall retrieval to find those documents whose constraints are satisfied by the data in the input query. (e.g. clinical trials)

Knowledge curation: 

1. **Wikichat**: question answering on a text corpus  
2. **co-Storm**: an interactive deep researcher to write a one-pager  
3. **DataTalk**: an interactive question-answering interface to databases  
4. **DataStorm**: insight discovery across documents and databases  
5. **Grill**: an interactive agent that can search and curate hundreds of scientific papers. 

Agent Harness: 

1. **Scientific reasoning with SHELDON:** Investigates problems with a dual search of the hypothesis and observation spaces. This has been shown to be more effective across diagnosis, abstract reasoning, and optimization benchmarks.   
2. **Efficient and scalable context management**: Automatically manages the context with automatically generated object-oriented data structures.  
3. **Accountable decision making with VERDICT**: Supports accountable decision making that returns a verifiable, faithful, consistent, and actionable rationale through the use of formal methods. VERDICT uses LLMs to translate documents into formal SMT formulae, enabling theorem provers to perform the complex logical tasks.  
4. **Long-horizon conversational management with Genie Worksheet**: Facilitates easy creation of long-horizon and long-context conversational task agents with a significantly higher completion rate than function calling.  Conversations are specified in a domain specific language (DSL) whose run-time manages the context to track conversations. This framework integrates data retrieval from free-text and structured data, along with programmable conversational agent policies. 

## Project Axes {#project-axes}

There are two major approaches to defining a project: you can start with an application area or a technique. But for the latter, you will still need a domain to test your techniques with. 

#### **A. Domain Driven**

1. What is your domain of interest?   
2. What is the challenge in your project? Examples:  
   1. The human-computer interaction dynamics  
   2. Precision, recall, accountability  
   3. Long context  
   4. Long horizon  
   5. Unknown — you may need to create the first prototype to discover the challenge  
3. Which techniques are you planning to use or planning to improve?   
4. How are you planning to evaluate your solution?

Let us consider the domain of medicine. OpenEvidence’s accuracy on diagnosis and treatment in the MedXpertQA benchmark is limited to 73%. How can we improve the accuracy?  Note that simply applying RAG to the pubmed literature is not going to work – because each paper reports just the findings of a particular experiment. Doctors rely on guidelines, which are created by experts in curating many papers on a topic. Using clinical diagnosis and treatment as an example, we will discuss a range of topics to help you think through the possibilities of your domain. 

**Scientific biomedical discoveries with experimentation.**  Which genes are responsible for drug resistance?  Starting with wet lab results which identify a long list of candidate genes, we need to scour the literature to prioritize the genes for further experimentation.  (Curation of knowledge). 

**Data-driven discoveries to inform new guidelines.** From the large number of patient records, can we find the causal inference between treatments and outcomes?  Causal inference requires us to identify possible confounders.  Can we identify potential confounders by studying the doctor’s notes in the patient records? Do doctors have suggestions on what are possible causal relations that we can test with real data? (Data-driven discovery). 

**Guidelines writing.** There are many medical publications, how do we synthesize all the findings? (Curation of knowledge)

**Application of Guidelines.** Diagnosis of rare diseases.  Doctors do not have the experience in diagnosing rare diseases, and must apply known knowledge (guidelines) to patient records.  (Decision making)

**Guidelines conformance:** Can we apply the guidelines to determine the treatment or to ensure that the doctor’s treatment confirms to the guidelines? (Decision making)

**Answering doctors’ queries on EHRs** (Electronic Health Records).  Can we provide clinical decision support by answering doctors’ queries. Can we answer complex queries such as why a certain treatment/test is ordered? What is the sequence of treatments? How long does it take for a disease to be diagnosed? Were there misdiagnoses in the patient’s history? What kind of drug side effects were experienced? (Question answering)

#### **B. Technique Driven**

You are welcome to improve the components of AOS or create new functionality to it. Here are some problems that we have identified: 

1. **CHURRO**: Currently there is no VLM that can handle the Chinese newspapers typeset in the 1800s and early 1900s (vertical characters, right-to-left reading, one article being split up in sections around the page). Two interesting directions are to fine-tune open-source models with synthesized training data or results generated by frontier models, which is too expensive for mass production.   
2. **SLIDERS:**  How to support sophisticated queries on patient records? Is it applicable to other domains?   
3. **SHELDON:** How to improve the management of hypotheses and report generation? Perform deep analysis across different domains: e.g. compilation of information of rare diseases; compilation of medical guidelines.  
4. **DataSTORM:** We have extended DataSTORM to perform reproducibility and analytic robustness of papers, and have applied it to 20 medical papers based on the large SEER data set of cancer patients. Can we apply this at scale to many more papers? and to other domains? Can we also use DataSTORM to extract new insights from the SEER dataset?    
5. **DataSTORM and SHELDON**: how do we integrate these two functions so that we can combine literature search with data analysis?    
6. **Genie-Worksheet:** Can we make it easier to build conversational agents using Genie Worksheets by automatically deriving worksheets from English descriptions or from the accessibility information on websites?   
7. **VERDICT**: Explore the use of accountability in other domains. In medicine: rare disease diagnosis, guideline adherence.  In finance: governance compliance.

### CHURRO-Synthesis: Domain-Adaptive OCR through Data Synthesis {#churro-synthesis:-domain-adaptive-ocr-through-data-synthesis}

**ID**: CHURRO-Synthesis  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Cyrus Zhou, CA; Andrew Biondo, CA  
**Question:** Can synthetic, quality-checked data improve open vision language models’ transcription of documents that require both layout understanding and interpretation of domain-specific text?  
**Motivation:** Historical manuscripts and newspapers are difficult to transcribe for two closely related reasons. Complex page layouts make it hard to identify regions and follow the correct reading order. Abbreviations, domain-specific acronyms, unfamiliar vocabulary, and ambiguous characters require context to interpret the text within those regions. We will study Latin manuscripts and Chinese newspapers, with the possibility of extending to Greek manuscripts.  
**Project:** Build a workflow that generates page transcriptions, region annotations, reading order, and uncertainty labels. Create examples that test layout understanding, context-dependent transcription, and cases where the two interact. Compare training an open model, using an agentic transcription workflow, and combining the two, with initial emphasis on the open model.  
**Prerequisites**: Python and experience working with image and text datasets. Familiarity with page layout analysis is helpful.  
**References**: Churro Paper: [https\://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1763](../readings/acl-2025-emnlp-main-1763.md); Churro code: [https\://github.com/stanford-oval/Churro](../repositories/stanford-oval-churro.md)  
**Quarter Goal**: Produce annotation guidelines and a quality-checked pilot dataset covering complex layouts, reading order, and context-dependent transcription. Run a small open-model training experiment and compare it with an agentic workflow on held-out pages, evaluating layout and transcription accuracy separately and together.  
**Status**: New

### CHURRO-Synthesis: Domain-Adaptive OCR through Distillation {#churro-synthesis:-domain-adaptive-ocr-through-distillation}

**ID**: CHURRO-Distillation  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Cyrus Zhou, CA; Andrew Biondo, CA  
**Question:** Can CHURRO learn from transcriptions produced by general-purpose agents, such as Claude Code or Codex, equipped with domain-specific skills?  
**Motivation:** An agent can use a skill that describes a collection’s script, abbreviations, layout conventions, and transcription rules. It can inspect a page and revise its transcription across multiple steps, but doing this for every page is costly. Distillation tests whether CHURRO can learn from those agent-produced examples and transcribe similar pages directly.  
**Project:** Develop a domain-specific skill and use an agent to produce transcriptions, region annotations, reading order, and uncertainty labels for pages from Latin manuscripts or Chinese newspapers, with Greek manuscripts as a possible extension. Review a subset of the annotations, then fine-tune CHURRO on the agent-produced data. Evaluate it on held-out pages, paying particular attention to complex layouts and abbreviations or ambiguous text that require semantic context. Compare quality and cost with the original CHURRO model and the agent workflow.  
**Prerequisites**: Python and experience with image and text datasets. Familiarity with model fine-tuning or historical document transcription is helpful.  
**References**: Churro Paper: https\://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1763; Churro code: https\://github.com/stanford-oval/Churro  
**Quarter Goal**:  Deliver a reusable domain-specific skill, a quality-checked pilot set of agent-produced annotations, a fine-tuned CHURRO checkpoint, and an evaluation of transcription quality and cost on held-out pages.  
**Status**: New

### Bridging the Human-Database Gap in Knowledge Base Access with Background Knowledge {#bridging-the-human-database-gap-in-knowledge-base-access-with-background-knowledge}

**ID**: Database-Knowledge-Gap  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Cyrus Zhou, CA  
**Question:** How can a text-to-SQL system use background knowledge to produce the SQL query that matches what a user actually means?  
**Motivation:** Users describe concepts in everyday language, while databases encode them through tables, codes, joins, and local conventions. A query can be syntactically valid yet answer the wrong question because the system misunderstood how a concept is represented in the database. Schema descriptions alone may not provide the knowledge needed to bridge that gap.  
**Project:** Study cases where several plausible SQL queries express different interpretations of a user’s question. Build a workflow that generates candidate queries, compares where they differ, and retrieves or elicits the background knowledge needed to choose among them. Evaluate whether this improves query correctness and whether the system can recognize cases that still require clarification from the user.  
**Prerequisites**: Python and SQL. Familiarity with language models or database systems is helpful.  
**References:** BEAVER paper: [https\://arxiv.org/abs/2409.02038](../readings/arxiv-2409-02038.md) ; BEAVER data and code: [https\://github.com/beaverbench/beaver](../repositories/beaverbench-beaver.md) ; AmbiQT and LogicalBeam: [https\://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.436/](../readings/acl-2023-emnlp-main-436.md) ; PRACTIQ: [https\://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.13/](../readings/acl-2025-naacl-long-13.md) ; Semantic Evaluation for Text-to-SQL with Distilled Test Suites: [https\://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.29/](../readings/acl-2020-emnlp-main-29.md) .  
**Quarter Goal**:  Assemble a small set of questions where background knowledge changes the correct SQL, implement a candidate-query comparison prototype, and measure its effect on query correctness against a text-to-SQL baseline.

### Tackling Long-Contexts with Down-Scaled Experimentation {#tackling-long-contexts-with-down-scaled-experimentation}

**ID**: Database-Down-Scaling  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Harshit Joshi, [harshitj@stanford.edu](mailto:harshitj@stanford.edu), CA  
**Question:** Can we use strategies learned from small tasks that fit within a language model’s context window to improve question answering on both small and much larger inputs?  
**Motivation:** A language model’s context window is the amount of information it can process in a single call. In our initial observations, models often perform well when all the information needed for a task fits within this window. A long-context pipeline, which breaks the input into pieces, processes them separately, and combines the results, can struggle on these same tasks. However, such pipelines become useful when the input is too large for one call, especially when it exceeds twice the model’s context window.  
This raises an interesting possibility: can we learn better ways to process large inputs by first experimenting on smaller versions of the same task? Small experiments are cheaper and easier to inspect. They also let us compare a pipeline’s answer with an answer produced by a model that sees the entire input at once.  
**Project:** Students will work with our SLIDERS, and its execution traces, which record the steps it takes to answer questions. They will study these traces on smaller tasks where the full input fits within a model’s context window, comparing SLIDERS with a single-shot model that sees the entire input. Using this comparison, students will identify where Sliders loses information or makes mistakes and develop strategies to improve its performance, such as selecting relevant passages, keeping better intermediate notes, or checking partial answers. They will then test whether strategies learned on small tasks improve Sliders on both unseen small tasks and larger inputs that exceed the model’s context window, measuring answer accuracy and processing cost.  
**Prerequisites**: Comfortable programming in Python, working with LLMs and datasets.  
**Quarter Goal**:   Develop a methodology for learning effective question-answering strategies on small tasks, using a single-shot model to guide improvements to Sliders. Test whether these strategies improve performance on unseen small tasks and transfer to inputs exceeding twice the model’s context window. Deliver an implementation and a short report comparing accuracy and processing cost, explaining when the methodology succeeds and where it falls short.

## Long Contexts (SLIDERS)  {#long-contexts-(sliders)}

### Why Was the Treatment Changed? Explaining Clinical Decisions in Longitudinal Patient Records {#why-was-the-treatment-changed?-explaining-clinical-decisions-in-longitudinal-patient-records}

**ID:** Patient-Records-Reasoning  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domains:**Biomedical     
**Mentor:** Harshit Joshi, [harshitj@stanford.edu](mailto:harshitj@stanford.edu)  
**Other advisors:** Priyank Shethia, pshethia@stanford.edu

**Question:** Can an AI system reconstruct why treatments were changed over a patient's history, by linking structured records with physicians' reasoning in clinical notes, and use the result for statistical and causal analysis of clinical practice?

**Motivation:** Hospital records document a patient's care over years: diagnoses, laboratory results, medications, procedures and visits, alongside the notes physicians write at each encounter. Structured data records that a treatment was started, stopped or changed, but the reasons are usually documented only in the notes, where physicians describe the findings they considered, the alternatives they weighed and the rationale for their decision. Linking these reasons to the structured timeline turns a patient record into an explained treatment history. At the scale of a health system, this makes it possible to study why treatment decisions are made, how long it takes to respond to new findings, and how practice varies across patients. It also supports observational causal analyses that adjust for the factors physicians actually considered, many of which are absent from structured data. These analyses can inform clinical research and practice. For physicians, they can show how decisions in their own institution compare with guidelines and with each other, and where responses to new findings are delayed. For researchers, recurring reasons for treatment changes and their associated outcomes suggest hypotheses for prospective studies and point to where guidelines are unclear. The target data are Stanford's STARR repository of deidentified patient records. Students develop and validate methods on a representative public subset in the same format, so that the pipeline can be applied to the full repository. 

**Project:**

1. **Patient timeline.** Build a chronological representation of each record that merges structured events (diagnoses, medications, labs, procedures) with the notes written at each encounter.  
2. **Treatment changes and reasons.** Identify treatment changes from medication records, and use SLIDERS to extract the physician's stated reasoning from the notes. SLIDERS processes long document collections chunk by chunk and handles records far longer than a model's context window. It induces a structured schema for the information to extract, such as the triggering findings, the alternatives considered and the rationale, and extracts evidence that links back to the note passages it came from. It then reconciles overlapping or conflicting accounts across notes, for instance when an admission note and a later follow-up give different reasons for the same change. Link each extracted reason to the events on the timeline that it refers to. Where a stated reason corresponds to structured data, such as a lab value or a new diagnosis, check that the data agree. Adapting SLIDERS to clinical notes, including time ordering, abbreviations and copied-forward text, is part of the project.  
3. **Statistical analysis.** Characterize treatment changes across patients: common reasons, time from a triggering finding to a change, and patient factors associated with different decisions.  
4. **Causal analysis.** For a chosen treatment decision, estimate its association with subsequent outcomes. Compare adjustment using structured confounders only against adjustment that also uses confounders extracted from notes. Results on the public subset serve to validate the method; population-level estimates come from applying the pipeline to STARR.  
5. **Evaluation.** Build a small annotated set of treatment changes and stated reasons on the public subset, and measure extraction accuracy. Use agreement with structured data as an additional check that needs no annotation.

**Prerequisites:** Python and experience with LLMs. Familiarity with SQL or data analysis is helpful. No clinical expertise is required.

**References:**

* STARR: Stanford Medicine Research Data Repository.  
* SLIDERS: Systematic Reviews via Automated Evidence Synthesis and Reconciliation. Joshi, Shethia, Dao, Lam. NeurIPS 2026\.

**Quarter Goal:** A pipeline that builds patient timelines, extracts treatment changes and the physicians' reasoning with evaluated accuracy, and runs statistical and causal analyses, validated on the public subset and ready to apply to STARR.

### Automatically Curating Cancer Treatment Guidelines from the Literature {#automatically-curating-cancer-treatment-guidelines-from-the-literature}

**ID:** Cancer-Guideline-Curation  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domains:**Biomedical

**Other Advisors:** Priyank Shethia, pshethia@stanford.edu

**Question:** Can an AI system curate treatment guidelines for a cancer type from the clinical literature, specifying for which patients and under which criteria treatment should be started, continued or changed, with each recommendation traceable to its supporting evidence?

**Motivation:** The clinical literature on cancer treatment is large and grows continuously. Each clinical study reports findings for a particular population, intervention and setting, and no single study tells a physician what to do for a given patient. Clinical practice guidelines fill this gap. They synthesize the evidence across many studies into recommendations that specify which patients a treatment applies to and under which criteria the course of treatment should change, and they grade the strength of the evidence behind each recommendation. Because guidelines are explicit and cite their evidence, experts can validate them, and practitioners can apply them directly in patient care. Producing them is expensive. ASCO's living guideline for hormone receptor-positive breast cancer required a 20-person panel three years and the review of thousands of publications to answer 17 questions. As a result, guidelines lag behind new evidence, and many questions and cancer types are not covered. Our clinical collaborators have recently curated guidelines for breast cancer and lung cancer, and their preprints provide expert references for developing and evaluating an automated system. A system developed on one cancer type and shown to work on another would be a pipeline that can curate and update guidelines for any cancer as new studies are published. In practice, such a pipeline could shorten the time between the publication of new evidence and its incorporation into guidelines, extend guidelines to cancer types and clinical questions that currently lack them, and make guideline development feasible for organizations that cannot convene large expert panels, with experts reviewing and validating generated recommendations rather than producing them from scratch.

**Project:**

1. **Target representation.** Using the breast cancer guideline as the development reference, define the structure of a recommendation: the patient population, the conditions or criteria that trigger it, the recommended action, the supporting studies and the strength of evidence.  
2. **Literature retrieval and screening.** Given a guideline question, retrieve candidate studies from PubMed and screen them for relevance. Measure recall against the studies the reference guideline cites.  
3. **Evidence extraction.** Use SLIDERS to extract structured evidence from the included studies, such as populations, interventions, comparators, outcomes and effect sizes, with each extracted item linked to the passage it came from. Reconcile overlapping or conflicting findings across studies.  
4. **Recommendation synthesis.** Synthesize the extracted evidence into recommendations in the target representation, with citations to the supporting studies and an assessment of evidence strength.  
5. **Evaluation.** Develop on the breast cancer guideline and test on the lung cancer guideline without further tuning. Compare the generated recommendations with the reference in terms of which questions are covered, whether the recommendations and their criteria agree, and whether the cited evidence overlaps. Where possible, have the collaborating clinicians review a sample of the generated recommendations.

**Prerequisites:** Python and experience with LLMs. Interest in medicine or evidence synthesis is helpful. No clinical expertise is required.

## **AOS Collaborative Workspaces**  {#aos-collaborative-workspaces}

### Shared State for Human–AI Teams {#shared-state-for-human–ai-teams}

**ID**: AOS-collaboration  
**Level:** **IntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Tommy Bruzzese (tbru@stanford.edu)

**Question:**  
How should AOS support persistent workspaces where multiple users and AI agents collaborate on file resources, annotations, and claims?

**Motivation:**  
Most agent systems are designed around a single user and an isolated conversational context, and most file systems are built around hierarchical storage — titles, folders, paths, and file types — as opposed to semantic, just-in-time retrieval of relevant resources. Research and knowledge work, however, is collaborative: teams work over shared data, contribute interpretations and annotations, disagree about claims, and gradually build a body of shared knowledge.

We can imagine AOS as consisting of two complementary parts: 

1) A “**DataBoss”** will serve as the persistent data and knowledge layer: managing the resources agents work over, including documents, datasets, metadata, annotations, derived claims, provenance, ownership, and permissions.   
2) The **AOS harness** will serve as the execution and orchestration layer: determining how agents operate over that state, including which models and tools they use, what context they retrieve, which resources they are allowed to access, and how their outputs are written back into AOS.

Supporting collaboration therefore requires work across both layers. DataBoss must be able to represent teams, shared and private collections, access control permissions, annotations, claims, and their provenance. The harness must make agents aware of this collaborative state and ensure that they retrieve, reason over, and modify only the resources that they/their user are authorized to access.

Important decision designs will arise as AI agents themselves contribute to the shared workspace. An AI-generated annotation or claim should not automatically become accepted team knowledge. The system should preserve who or what created it, what evidence supports it, whether it was edited or accepted by a human, and whether other collaborators disagree.

Examples of use cases are historians using HistoryGenie or, down the line, medical teams working to parse through reams of research together. The underlying collaboration primitives should be general to AOS and reusable across many domains.

**Project:**  
Design and implement a collaborative workspace layer for AOS spanning both DataBoss and the agent harness.

On the **DataBoss** side, the project may include:

* Consistent collaboration/sharing/ACL primitives  
  * A domain-agnostic collaboration API for applications and agents  
* Persistent human- and AI-generated annotations  
  * Claims linked to supporting evidence and source resources  
* Provenance recording who or what created, edited, accepted, or rejected a contribution  
* Representation of competing or conflicting interpretations  
  * Durable discussion and annotation state associated with resources and collections

On the **harness** side, the project may include:

* Permission-aware retrieval (and ensuring agents cannot retrieve or modify resources outside their permissions)  
* Supplying agents with the appropriate team- and user-specific context  
* Allowing agents to create, update, or respond to annotations and claims  
* Reasoning over evolving collaborative state, including accepted knowledge, unresolved questions, and disagreement

**Some leading research questions:**

- Can an agent distinguish between one researcher's private hypothesis and a claim accepted by the broader team?   
- Can it identify when collaborators disagree about an interpretation?   
- Can it update its answers after a human corrects an AI-generated claim?   
- Can several agents operate over the same workspace without producing duplicated, inconsistent, or unauthorized state?

**Prerequisites**:  
Experience with web or systems engineering; familiarity with databases and LLM-based agents. Experience with collaborative software, access-control systems, agent orchestration, or HCI (human-computer interaction) is a plus. While the semantic filesystem will likely not have a typical GUI, UI design or UI experience work is a plus.

**Quarter Goal**:  
A working collaborative workspace layer integrated across DataBoss and the AOS harness. Teams should be able to share collections with permission controls, create persistent human- and AI-generated annotations or claims, and allow agents to retrieve and reason over this shared state while respecting provenance and access restrictions. The system should be demonstrated in at least one domain application, such as HistoryGenie.

## Scientific Reasoning Harness (SHELDON) {#scientific-reasoning-harness-(sheldon)}

### Reliable AI Agents for Multi-Step Scientific Analysis {#reliable-ai-agents-for-multi-step-scientific-analysis}

**ID:** SHELDON-Runtime  
**Level: IntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science    
**Mentor:** Harshit Joshi, harshitj@stanford.edu  
**Other Advisors:** Priyank Shethia, pshethia@stanford.edu

**Question:** Can hypothesis-driven investigation make AI agents accurate and consistent on real scientific analyses that take many dependent steps, from raw data to a final answer or artifact?

**Motivation:** Many scientific data analysis tasks require a sequence of dependent steps: preprocessing the data, selecting and running tools, inspecting intermediate results and choosing subsequent analyses. Examples include identifying a knocked-out gene from RNA-seq data and correcting contamination in single-cell data. Errors in early steps propagate to later ones, and several analysis paths are often plausible at each step. Current agents perform poorly on such tasks. General coding agents append each tool output to their context and continue along a single line of analysis. As the context grows, their exploration narrows, and alternative explanations are rarely revisited once an early choice has been made. Agents that perform these analyses reliably would let researchers analyze and reanalyze public datasets at a scale that is not feasible manually, and would make AI-assisted results easier to trust. SHELDON is a scientific reasoning agent that maintains a set of competing hypotheses, gathers observations that discriminate between them, and records which observations support or refute each hypothesis. It outperforms coding and research agents on diagnosis, root-cause analysis and optimization benchmarks. In those benchmarks, however, the solution is a single output that is revised in each round. This project applies SHELDON's approach to multi-step analyses in a computing environment, where the state of the environment and intermediate results carry over between steps. It evaluates the resulting systems on both accuracy and consistency across repeated runs.

**Project:** Each team picks one benchmark. Teams first run general coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, Terminus 2\) and SHELDON unchanged under the same conditions, repeat each task several times, and record both accuracy and consistency. They then adapt SHELDON to the task type and compare again.

*A. Answer-shaped tasks: BioMysteryBench*

* **Tasks:** 99 bioinformatics questions written by domain experts, where metadata has been removed from real data and the agent must recover it through analysis. Examples include:  
  * "What gene was knocked out in the experimental samples compared to control samples based on RNA-seq data?"  
  * "Which human organ is this single-cell RNA-seq dataset derived from?"  
  * "From whole-genome sequences, which sample is the mother of sample X and which is the father?"  
  * "Given H3K27ac ChIP-seq peaks from an unknown cell type, identify the cell type."  
* **Why it fits:** the answer is short, but reaching it takes many analysis steps. Hypotheses are candidate answers, and observations come from analyses run in a bioinformatics sandbox.  
* **Adaptation:** let SHELDON run tools and keep data, intermediate files and results across rounds, so each round of investigation builds on the last.

*B. Artifact-shaped tasks: Terminal-Bench-Science (advanced)*

* **Tasks:** 70 research workflows across life, physical, earth, mathematical and engineering sciences, each producing an artifact checked by tests the task's author wrote. Examples include:  
  * inferring missing IBD status, inflammation, tissue site and sex for blinded samples across six intestinal-biopsy cohorts  
  * correcting ambient RNA contamination in a three-batch single-cell experiment without erasing a rare cell state  
  * writing a program that separates planet transits from eclipsing binaries and instrumental noise in TESS light curves  
* **Why it's harder:** the output is a data product or program built over many steps, and regenerating it from scratch every round is impractical.  
* **Adaptation:** design a runtime that lets SHELDON reason over the workflow. Possible directions:  
  * break the task into decision points, such as a preprocessing choice or a method choice, and run the hypothesis–observation loop on each one  
  * checkpoint the environment, so competing choices can be tried on branches of the same state and compared  
  * treat intermediate outputs (files, logs, plots, tables) as observations linked to the hypotheses they support or refute

**Prerequisites:** Python, experience with LLM agents, and comfort working in Linux and Docker environments. Scientific computing experience is helpful. No domain expertise is required.

**References:**

* Sheldon: Reasoning through Dual Search over Hypotheses and Observation Spaces. Joshi, Shethia, Lam (submitted).  
* Generalization Beyond Coding: An Empirical Comparison of Coding Agents and Purpose-Built Agents. Sang, Liu, Ellis, Lam (submitted).  
* BioMysteryBench: [https\://www\.anthropic.com/research/Evaluating-Claude-For-Bioinformatics-With-BioMysteryBench](../project-references/www-anthropic-com-research-evaluating-claude-for-bioinformatics-with-biomysterybench.md)  
* Terminal-Bench-Science: [https\://github.com/harbor-framework/terminal-bench-science](../project-references/terminal-bench-science-readme.md)

**Quarter Goal:** For each team, baseline results for coding agents and unmodified SHELDON on its benchmark, an adapted version of SHELDON for that task type, and a comparison of accuracy and consistency across repeated runs, with an analysis of where each design choice helps.

### Efficient Data Sampling {#efficient-data-sampling}

**ID:** efficient-data-sampling  
**Level:** Intermediate  
**Domain:** Computer Science

**Question:** Can we make prompt and harness optimization cheaper, more stable, and more effective by being smarter about which data we use for training (reflection) and for evaluation?

**Motivation:** Current prompt and harness optimizers (e.g., GEPA, Meta-Harness) use an LLM as a reflector that proposes and edits candidates. These methods are often unstable because both the training signal and the evaluation scores are noisy: the agent being optimized is highly stochastic, so a candidate can look better or worse purely by chance. In addition, evaluation typically accounts for roughly 80% of the total cost of an optimization run. Reducing it makes each search step much cheaper, which lets the search run longer and compound its gains.

**Project:** The student will study data-sampling strategies grounded in the statistical structure of the data and of the agent’s behavior, and measure how each affects the performance, cost, and stability of existing optimizers downstream. We focus on three questions:

1. Training data: how should examples be sampled and presented to the reflector so that learning is smooth and stable?  
2. Predictable instances: how can we reliably detect instances whose outcomes are predictable (e.g., consistently solved or consistently failed across candidates), so they can be evaluated less often or skipped?  
3. Noisy, decisive instances: how can we reliably detect instances that are noisy yet most likely to decide which candidate gets selected, so the evaluation budget (e.g., extra rollouts) can be concentrated on them?

### Meta-Optimization using Semantic Clustering and Abstraction {#meta-optimization-using-semantic-clustering-and-abstraction}

**Level:** Intermediate Advanced  
**Domain:** Computer Science  
**Mentor:** Jiuding Sun, [sunjd24@stanford.edu](mailto:sunjd24@stanford.edu)  
**Other Advisors:** Priyank Shethia, pshethia@stanford.edu

**Question:** Can a hierarchical, semantically clustered view of a dataset and its execution traces, maintained throughout optimization, help prompt and harness optimizers make changes that generalize rather than changes that fit individual examples?

**Motivation:** Prompt and harness optimizers such as GEPA and Meta-Harness improve an LLM system on a given dataset. They run the current candidate, inspect the scores and execution traces, and have an LLM propose revisions. At any step the optimizer sees only a small minibatch or a handful of traces, so revisions tend to target the examples in view and can miss patterns that only show up across many examples. As the candidate improves, the set of failure modes also changes: some are resolved and new ones appear.

**Project:**

2. Design and build a meta-optimizer around a hierarchical abstraction layer over the dataset and the optimizer's traces. Clusters combine input type and failure mode and are updated as the candidate changes. Each summary links to its member traces, so the optimizer can drill down from patterns to raw evidence.  
3. Explore how the optimizer uses this layer. Possible directions:

### Logic as a Board Game {#logic-as-a-board-game}

**ID:** logic-board-game  
**Level:** Intermediate, Advanced  
**Domain:** Computer Science, Mathematics  
**Mentor:** Jiuding Sun ([sunjd24@stanford.edu](mailto:sunjd24@stanford.edu))  
**Other Advisors:** TBD  
**Prerequisites:** Experience with LLM training (SFT and RL). Familiarity with self-play RL (e.g., AlphaZero) or with logic and proofs is a plus.  
**Question:** Played strictly by its inference rules, first-order logic is a board game: every proof step is a move, and every position has finitely many legal moves. Can an agent master this game through self-play, posing its own conjectures and inventing the lemmas needed to prove them?  
**Motivation:** Most self-play work on mathematics runs on Lean, where a move is a free-form tactic drawn from an effectively unbounded space, not one of finitely many legal moves as in a board game. Mathematics also has no fixed goal, so the agent must pose its own conjectures: ones that are hard but still provable for it (intrinsic motivation). **Project:** Students will focus on RL training in our own first-order RL environment, which comes with a human-written library. Every move applies an inference rule strictly, so each position has finitely many legal moves, and each move is checked almost instantly.

1. Self-play. On one sub-theory, the agent poses conjectures, plays out proofs move by move, and trains on what it proves. The human-written library seeds the process.  
2. Lemma invention. This is the one open-ended move: the agent learns when to introduce a new lemma and which of its earlier results to build on.  
3. Evaluation. Compare against the same agent without lemma invention at matched compute, measuring proof depth and success on held-out theorems from the human-written library.

### **HistoryGenie: Building a Historical Knowledge Layer with SLIDERS** {#historygenie:-building-a-historical-knowledge-layer-with-sliders}

**ID:** HG-Sliders  
**Level:** **IntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain:** History  
**Mentor:** Tommy Bruzzese (tbru@stanford.edu)  
**Other Advisors:** Trevor Getz (Professor of African and World History at SFSU) (tgetz@sfsu.edu)  
**Prerequisites:** Experience with LLMs; interest in history/humanities is a plus

**Question:** Does SLIDERS perform well on large collections of historical documents? How can it be adapted to increase performance?

**Motivation:** Historical archives contain enormous amounts of information about people, places, institutions, communities, languages, events, and relationships, but most of this knowledge is embedded in unstructured text.

We have built [HistoryGenie](../project-references/history-genie-stanford-edu.md), a platform that allows historians to upload and organize historical documents into collections, review and correct transcriptions, search and chat across their documents with LLMs, and collaborate through shared team access. It is currently used by groups of historians studying Southern Italy, South Africa, and will support undergraduate history courses at Stanford later this year. 

HistoryGenie uses [CHURRO](https://docs.google.com/document/d/17GsPD8IzaSJ_MnGj-yqZgw1MG65bohxeTHeFqz68hi4/edit?tab=t.0#heading=h.6ty58v5e4gbw), our open-weight vision-language model for historical text recognition, to make difficult historical prints and manuscripts machine-readable. Once these documents have been transcribed, however, historians still face a problem: how can we systematically reason over the information contained across hundreds or thousands of documents?

Given a research question and document corpus, SLIDERS can automatically induce a structured evidence schema, extract source-grounded evidence, normalize and reconcile overlapping or conflicting records, and consolidate information across documents. In principle, these capabilities should support many of the core functions of a historical knowledge layer, including recognizing variant references to the same person, extracting relationships among people and institutions, organizing dates and places, and preserving the textual evidence behind each structured claim.

The open question is how robust the SLIDERS mechanisms are when applied to historical archives. Historical documents have particularly unique difficulties: names may appear with very variant spellings or transcription errors; the same person may be referred to by surname, title, abbreviation, or role; places and institutions may change names over time; identities may remain genuinely uncertain; relationships are often expressed indirectly; and many historically significant entities may not appear in modern knowledge bases. 

**Project:** Integrate SLIDERS into HistoryGenie and investigate how well its existing extraction and reconciliation pipeline works on historical collections. Students will use historical documents as a domain-specific stress test: identifying where SLIDERS works already, where it fails, and what changes are necessary to make the system more robust for historical research and for deployment as an agent within AOS.

Several research questions are especially important:

**1\. Historical entity extraction and resolution.**  
How well does SLIDERS recognize that references such as “Dr. X,” “X,” and a misspelled form of X refer to the same historical person? Can it avoid incorrectly merging different people with similar names? How should the system represent ambiguous or unresolved identities rather than forcing a canonical answer?

**2\. Evidence-grounded relationship extraction.**  
Can the structured representation move beyond recording that two entities occur in a document and reliably extract propositions such as *X worked for Y*, *X traveled to Z*, *A disputed land with B*, or *this document discusses language L*? Can every relationship remain traceable to the passage or passages that support it?

**3\. Robustness of SLIDERS for historical corpora.**  
SLIDERS was designed as a general structured evidence-synthesis system rather than specifically for archival material. Which parts of its schema induction, extraction, reconciliation, and confidence/uncertainty handling break when applied to noisy historical documents? What modifications improve its performance? If nothing breaks, that is an important result as well to have systematically proven.

**Quarter Goal:** A working integration of SLIDERS into HistoryGenie, together with an evaluation on real historical documents with history Professors and students that identifies where the existing pipeline succeeds and where historical material requires new methods or modifications. Ideally, the project will contribute improvements back to the SLIDERS/AOS ecosystem while giving historians a usable structured knowledge layer over their collections.

**Status:** Continuing

**References:**

1. *SLIDERS: Systematic Reviews via Automated Evidence Synthesis and Reconciliation*  
   Harshit Joshi, Priyank Shethia, Jadelynn Dao, Monica S. Lam. 2026\.

### Benchmark Monitor {#benchmark-monitor}

**ID**: benchmark-monitor  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediateAdvanced**  
**Domain**: Computer Science  
**Mentor:** [Shicheng Liu](mailto:sliu22@stanford.edu)  
**Other Advisors:** Jiacheng Sang, jiacheng.sang@duke.edu  
**Question:**  
Can we build a benchmark leaderboard that keeps track of the latest SOTA on a benchmark?

**Motivation:**  If you have done ML research, you know the pain when you want to evaluate your approach on a benchmark, and just don’t know what the benchmark’s latest State-of-the-Art (SOTA) is. A new approach could have emerged in the last month, last quarter, or the last year, and you are not sure whether the SOTA number you have at hand is still accurate.

Papers with Code used to fill this role, but it was shut down in July 2025\. Services such as Artificial Analysis ([https\://artificialanalysis.ai/](../project-references/artificialanalysis-ai.md)) run their own evaluations of models. None of them tracks the results that published papers report on a benchmark.

**Project:** In this project, we aim to build a benchmark monitor that tracks the latest SOTA on each benchmark, and open it up to the NLP/ML community for collective improvements. Every number on the monitor must be verifiable. The pipeline must not only extract a score correctly but also cite its evidence: the source paper, the exact table and row, and a verbatim excerpt. This lets us catch model hallucinations. A score whose excerpt does not appear in the paper can be rejected automatically, and anyone can check the remaining scores against the source. A matching excerpt is not enough on its own, though: a reviewer still has to confirm that it supports the claimed evaluation setting.

This project will involve the following aspects, and you can choose to address any 2-3 of them in-depth:

1. Can we build a benchmark for this task of finding the latest SOTA? This task is harder than you might think first: benchmarks often have different subsets and evaluation metrics, and papers working on a particular benchmark may also choose to evaluate it with a slightly different setting or on a different subset. Building a benchmark to evaluate LLM’s aperformance on this task would need to address all these problems effectively.  
2. After you construct a benchmark, can we build a harness better optimized for this task than Claude Code / Codex? This involves first evaluating off-the-shelf harnesses on this task and identify their weaknesses (e.g. parsing and interpreting tables in published papers), and work on improvements on them.   
3. Agentic systems can only get us so far. A good system could mean continued inputs from the NLP/ML community. How do we build a good interface that enables easier community inputs? For instance, the benchmark authors themselves can leave comments as to whether a particular number or evaluation criteria is correct. And How do we automatically judge and turn a comment into a correction that cites its evidence (paper URL, table location, verbatim excerpt) without silently overwriting the original record? This tool could be very useful for the community, serving as an updated version of the deprecated “paper with code”

2. Timmer, Hou, and Wan. 2025\. A Position Paper on the Automatic Generation of Machine Learning Leaderboards. EMNLP. arXiv:2505.17465.

### Seeing What No One in the OR Has Time to Watch: Multi-Role Agent Discussions on Sterile-Field Risk {#seeing-what-no-one-in-the-or-has-time-to-watch:-multi-role-agent-discussions-on-sterile-field-risk}

**ID**: Sterile-Field-CoSTORM  
**Level: BeginnerIntermediate**  
**Domain**: Biomedical  
**Mentor:** Jeongyeon Kim ([jeongyeon.kim@stanford.edu](mailto:jeongyeon.kim@stanford.edu)), CS PhD, CA  
**Other Advisors:** Dr. Amanatullah, Orthopaedic Surgery, Stanford School of Medicine / Dr. Lifeng Fan, Research Scientist, Cognitive Reasoning Lab, Beijing Institute for General Artificial Intelligence (BIGAI)

**Question:** Can a discussion among AI agents representing the different roles in an operating room turn hours of surgical video into an understanding of sterile-field risk that hospital leaders can act on, including where the stakeholders agree and where they do not?

**Motivation:** Hospital administrators decide how operating rooms are staffed, how traffic is controlled, and what each team is trained on. Sterile-field breaches are among the risks those decisions should address, because they bear directly on surgical site infection. Each lasts under a second, and they come from every role in the room. Administrators were not in the room, cannot watch hours of video, and do not know which questions to ask. Nor is there a single answer to give them.  
Many of these events are near-misses whose status is itself a judgment. How close is too close, and was a movement a breach or a necessary part of someone's work? A surgeon, a perioperative nurse, an infection-prevention specialist and a device representative, each trained to different standards, can read the same moment differently. And any response trades one stakeholder's priority against another's. Fewer door openings lower contamination risk but slow the retrieval of supplies; tighter traffic rules protect the sterile field but add to nursing workload. An administrator therefore needs to see where these perspectives agree, which points to clear risks to act on now, and where they diverge, which points to standards that must first be negotiated.  
We recorded three real knee replacement surgeries, annotated 520 sterile-field events by role, phase, and zone, and collected a surgeon's commentary on what went wrong and how it should have been handled.

**Project:** Co-STORM lets a user observe and steer a discussion among perspective-guided expert agents and a moderator, and organizes what is uncovered into a mind map.

* **Experts.** Instantiate the experts as the stakeholders in the operating room: surgeon, scrub tech, circulating nurse, device representative, anesthesia, and infection prevention. Ground every utterance in the annotated events, the surgeon's commentary, and linked video clips.  
* **Agreement and disagreement.** The discussion makes explicit where the roles agree and where they diverge, both on how serious an event is and on the trade-offs of possible responses.  
* **Moderator.** The moderator raises what has not yet been discussed, such as a recurring cross-role interaction during setup. Because the record shows what happened but not why, the discussion never asserts causes; possible explanations are raised as open questions for the OR staff to confirm. All discussion stays at the level of roles, phases, and workflows, never individuals, consistent with the dataset's ethics.  
* **Evaluation.** Use Co-STORM's discourse-turn rubrics, coverage of the surgeon's key points, and grounding accuracy of every cited claim. Have practitioners from each role judge whether the agents represent their perspective faithfully, and whether the agreements and disagreements surfaced match their own. Compare role-grounded experts against generic experts and a RAG chatbot, and run the original ablations (without moderator, without multiple experts).  
* **Interface.** Build an interface for hospital administrators. They can follow and steer the discussion, browse the mind map, see at a glance where the roles agree and diverge, filter by surgery, phase, and role, and open the video clip behind every cited claim.

**Prerequisites**: Python, experience with LLM agents. Interest in healthcare quality and safety. No surgical expertise required.

### Literature-Informed Experiment Selection for Cancer Drug Combinations {#literature-informed-experiment-selection-for-cancer-drug-combinations}

**ID:** Cancer-Combo-Agent  
**Level:** Advanced  
**Domain:** Biomedical  
**Proposed advisor:** Dr. Bryant Lin  
**Level: Advanced**  
**Domain**: Biomedical  
**Mentor:** Bryant Lin, MD  
**Other Advisors:** Minseung Choi, Janina Troper  
**Question:** Can an agent that combines literature evidence with a statistical model find effective drug combinations for cancer cells in fewer experiments than standard active learning, and can its proposed mechanisms generate correct, testable predictions about which cancer cells will respond?

**Motivation:** Cancers that become resistant to one drug are often treated with combinations, but the number of possible pairs is far too large to test. Labs pick experiments by intuition and literature reading, one batch at a time. Standard active-learning methods pick experiments from numbers alone and ignore what is known about drug mechanisms. This project tests whether an agent that reasons like a scientist \- proposing hypotheses from the literature, then updating after each result \- finds good combinations faster. It also asks whether the agent's proposed mechanisms generate accurate predictions about which cells will respond. The agent is benchmarked on a simulated lab: a dataset where every result has already been measured but is hidden until the agent "runs" that experiment.

**Project:** Data. Use drug-combination screens from Therapeutics Data Commons and the DrugComb portal, restricted to cancer cell lines. Pool the cancer lines and fix a candidate pool of drug pairs.  
Simulated lab. Start with a set of drug-cell-line experiments that have measured results. Hide those results from the agent. In each round, the agent selects a small number of experiments to “run,” sees their results, and uses them to decide which experiments to run next.  
Agent. A numerical response model with uncertainty estimates proposes candidates. The LLM retrieves mechanism evidence (SHELDON), proposes and critiques hypotheses, and chooses the next batch. Statistical tools, not the LLM, estimate responses.  
Explanations. For each combination the agent flags as promising, it writes an explanation and labels its level: mechanistic (a specific pathway in these cells), cross-cancer (worked in other tumor types), or statistical (similar drugs behaved this way). For mechanistic explanations, the agent must state a testable prediction about which cell lines should respond, based on the proposed mechanism.  
Leakage control. Keep hidden measurements, and any paper reporting those exact screen results, out of the agent's evidence. Measure separately how the agent does on famous combinations versus obscure ones, since the LLM may already know the famous ones.

**Evaluation:** Discovery efficiency: number of strong combinations found for a fixed experiment budget, compared against random selection, uncertainty sampling, and Bayesian optimization.  
Two outcomes: report both synergy (the pair does better than expected from each drug alone) and overall growth inhibition (the pair kills cells well), since they answer different questions.  
Explanations: test whether the predictions implied by the agent's proposed mechanisms hold on held-out cell lines.

**Prerequisites**: Python, machine learning (Bayesian optimization or active learning is a strong plus), and experience building LLM agents. Basic cancer biology is helpful.

**References**: Therapeutics Data Commons (Huang et al., 2021); DrugComb (Zagidullin et al., 2019; Zheng et al., 2021); NCI-ALMANAC (Holbeck et al., 2017); O'Neil et al., 2016; SHELDON; DataSTORM (Liu et al., COLM 2026).

A structured, cited database would show researchers and drug developers which resistance states exist in patients, how common each is, and which have been studied in lab models. Building it is a strong test of SLIDERS. The corpus is large, each paper contributes a small piece, and the hard problems are merging duplicates, reconciling conflicting numbers, and never inventing a citation. **Project:** **Scope.** Start with EGFR-targeted drugs (osimertinib and earlier EGFR inhibitors), where resistance is best studied. Add KRAS G12C inhibitors (sotorasib, adagrasib) if time allows.

3. **Reconcile.** Merge patients reported in more than one paper, combine resistance mechanism frequency estimates across studies while keeping each source visible, and flag conflicts instead of picking one number.  
4. **Publish.** Deliver the database as a sortable spreadsheet, with every row linked to its source.

## Accountable Clinical Decision Making  {#accountable-clinical-decision-making}

with Case Studies in Clinical Trial Matching and Rare Disease Diagnosis

**Monica S. Lam     Euan Ashley    James Ford    Emily Alsentzer     James Dickerson**

### 

Abstract

Artificial intelligence holds immense promise for medicine, but it cannot be fully trusted in high-stakes clinical decisions until each answer is explained and verified. In critical tasks like clinical trial matching and rare disease diagnosis, undetectable AI mistakes can cost lives. 

This project pioneers a new standard of **accountability** in clinical AI by developing **VERDICT**, a neurosymbolic system that combines large language models with formal mathematical reasoning.  By translating medical data and rules into precise formal representations, and reasoning over them with proven formal solvers, we enable AI to deliver decisions that are not just accurate, but also **verifiable, faithful, consistent, and actionable**. Every recommendation comes with a transparent rationale, detailing the supporting evidence, key assumptions, and pivotal conditions that could change the outcome, so clinicians can inspect, trust, and act with confidence. Leveraging VERDICT, we also derive counterfactual training samples to train large language models with stronger native accountability. 

Demonstrated first in **clinical trial matching** and **rare disease diagnosis**, and extensible to other domains, our approach lays the foundation for a new era of accountable AI: one where every high-stakes recommendation is dependable, transparent, and ready for real-world care. 		

**Background**

Many high-stakes clinical decisions require evaluating complex considerations using patient records at scale, while the contained information is often incomplete. For example, while ClinicalTrials.gov lists more than 500,000 trials \[1\], the difficulty of finding eligible patients satisfying complex criteria causes roughly 80% of trials to miss their initial enrollment targets or timelines \[2\]. In rare-disease diagnosis, many of the 7,000+ recognized diseases \[3\] are unfamiliar to any single clinician, and patients often remain undiagnosed for years \[4\].

Current AI systems are not *accountable* enough to support decisions of subsequent clinical management. Simple labels such as “not eligible” or “top diagnosis: X” are inadequate; they need to indicate the evidence used, the assumptions made in the face of missing data, whether similar cases would be treated similarly, and what recourse can be taken to change the decision. This kind of **case-level accountability** is essential in medicine, and other high-stakes domains, including governance, law, and 

 \[5\].

Existing approaches fall short of supporting accountable decision-making. Embedding-based retrieval methods treat criteria as soft signals rather than enforceable constraints, limiting both precision and transparency\[6\]\[7\]. LLM-based systems are found to apply policies inconsistently across equivalent cases\[8\]\[9\] and provide rationales that are not faithful to the actual basis of the decision\[10\]\[11\]\[12\]. Models trained on knowledge graphs do not expose why a candidate was accepted, rejected, or ranked highly\[13\]. 

**Problem Statement**

This project asks how to make AI decision-making accountable enough for high-stakes use. Decisions are considered ***accountable*** if they are: ***verifiable***, by providing verifiable logic tied to the source record and decision criteria so errors can be detected; ***faithful***, by surfacing the assumptions required to reach a decision so experts can override them or supply missing information; ***consistent***, across equivalent cases; and ***actionable***, by identifying pivotal conditions whose change could alter the outcome. Note that **trustworthiness** and **accountability** are related but distinct. Trustworthy AI aims to improve the average reliability of model outputs. Accountable AI must support correct handling of each individual case, not just good average performance. 

**Key Hypothesis**

Our key hypothesis is that **formal methods can make accountability a structural property of AI decision-making**. To test this, we pursue two aims: 

**(1)** We will develop **VERDICT**, a general neurosymbolic clinical decision-maker that produces decisions with **verifiable, faithful, consistent, and actionable** rationales from patient records and a set of textual decision criteria. We demonstrate the generality of VERDICT with two case studies on real patient data: in **clinical trial matching**, a patient is matched to a clinical trial according to the trial’s eligibility constraints and patient preferences; in **rare-disease diagnosis**, a patient is matched to a rare disease if they exhibit phenotypes and genes associated with that disease. VERDICT will return not only a decision, but also a **verifiable account of its basis**: why a case matches, what assumptions were required, or what pivotal conditions would need to change for the decision to flip. Such accountability is important because, for example, it is not possible to match a patient to a rare disease perfectly.  

**(2)** We will study how to improve the **native accountability of LLMs** by experimenting with model fine-tuning using VERDICT for **counterfactual distillation of accountable decision behavior**.

**Core Idea**

Our key idea is to separate language understanding from decision making. We use an LLM to semantically parse decision criteria and patient records into formal **SMT (Satisfiability Modulo Theories)** representations and a solver to compute the decision from that representation. **The key innovation** is that **semantic parsing** and **formal representation** turn unstructured language into an **auditable substrate for decision-making. Formalization forces disclosure.** An LLM asked directly for a decision can silently resolve ambiguity or missing values\[14\]; an LLM asked to produce a formal encoding cannot. We make explicit every ambiguity, temporal condition, exception, or missing value, by leveraging the SNOMED medical model. Once formalized, the **SMT**\[15\] and **MaxSAT (Maximum Satisfiability)**\[16\]\[17\]\[18\] solvers can produce verifiable results that support accountability: explicit traces and residual constraints reveal the assumptions on which a decision rests; equivalent formal representations yield equivalent outcomes; and identify the minimal conditions whose change would flip the result. Because the solvers are verifiably correct, errors are localized to semantic parsing, where they can be directly interpreted and corrected by inspecting the **SMT** representation.

**Preliminary Evidence**

In clinical trial matching, we showed that LLMs grounded in medical ontologies can convert ambiguity, implicit assumptions, and missing data into explicit SMT constraints. On the TREC 2022 benchmark with synthesized patient vignettes, **VERDICT** achieves about **90%** match accuracy, comparable to a well-tuned LLM-based matcher, while providing **substantially stronger accountability**: physicians can inspect the assumptions behind a decision and identify what facts would change it. To handle matching at scale, we have developed a novel method of relevant trial retrieval with significantly higher precision and recall by projecting SMT constraints into relational algebra \[6\]. Retrieval is fast, requiring 0.15 seconds per patient over 3,621 trials. 

**Research Plan** 

**Aim 1: Neurosymbolic accountable decision-making** 

We will extend VERDICT to handle real patient records and apply the same accountability pipeline to **clinical trial matching** and **rare-disease diagnosis**. We will evaluate clinical trial matching on real patient records from **Stanford** and **Mayo Clinic**, and rare-disease diagnosis on real-world cohorts from the **Undiagnosed Diseases Network**\[18\] and the **Deciphering Developmental Disorders**\[19\] study. We will compare against state-of-the-art systems and assess accuracy and  accountability, using automated metrics and **clinician evaluation of whether outputs are understandable, contestable, and actionable in clinical workflow**. These evaluations will establish an **accountability benchmark** for clinical decision-making, grounded in both formal verification and clinician use.

**Aim 2: LLM improvement through counterfactual distillation of accountable decision behavior.** 

Rather than teaching an LLM merely to imitate outputs, we will use **VERDICT** to teach the **formal decision boundary**—when a decision should change, when it should remain stable, and when it should defer. VERDICT makes this possible by generating **solver-defined counterfactual neighborhoods**, including minimal changes that must flip a decision, changes that should leave it unchanged, and missing-information variants that should trigger clarification or explicit assumptions. We will train open-weight LLMs first to produce grounded formal representations and explicit assumptions, and then to generate decisions and accountable rationales. We will test two hypotheses: **(1)** that **learning from formally-defined counterfactual structure improves accountability** more effectively than training on labels or rationales alone, and **(2)** that **accountable decision structure transfers** across tasks, such that training on clinical trial matching improves accountable reasoning in rare-disease diagnosis. Evaluation will be conducted on the accountability benchmarks established in Aim 1\.

This project addresses a core barrier to AI in medicine: the lack of transparent, reliable support for high-stakes decisions. By making AI recommendations in clinical trial matching and rare disease diagnosis both verifiable and clearly explained, we empower clinicians and health systems to rely on AI with new confidence. Ultimately, this work not only improves clinical workflows and patient outcomes, but also establishes a new paradigm of accountability for AI in critical domains.

## AOS: An Open, Accountable Agentic OS for Long-Horizon Workflows {#aos:-an-open,-accountable-agentic-os-for-long-horizon-workflows}

Monica Lam, Computer Science Department, School of Engineering

Jennifer Caswell-Jin, Department of Oncology, School of Medicine

Stanford University

Motivation. Large language models (LLMs) and AI-powered agents are poised to profoundly transform clinical practice and medical research. Systems like Open Evidence are already used by more than 60% of U.S. clinicians \[1\]. Yet despite this rapid uptake, significant limitations remain \[2\]\[3\]\[4\]. For example, the advanced Deep Consult model within Open Evidence achieves only 73% accuracy on the MedXpertQA benchmark \[5\]. Similarly, platforms like TrialGPT \[6\] still fall short on precision and recall in clinical trial matching. Most critically, current systems often lack accountability and struggle to provide rationales that are grounded, faithful, consistent, and actionable \[7\]\[8\]\[9\].

To realize the potential of AI in medical research, we must address not just accuracy, but also the challenges of scale, complexity, and collaboration. Specifically, Dr. Jennifer Caswell-Jin, a co-PI of this proposal, recently led a three-year, 20-person effort to develop ASCO’s first living clinical practice guideline in breast cancer, which required the review of thousands of publications to answer only 17 questions \[10\]. AI could dramatically accelerate the creation, updating, and dissemination of such guidelines for a much broader range of questions and medical fields—not only generating evidence-based answers at scale, but also suggesting treatments to clinicians aligned with the latest guidelines. Achieving this vision requires systems that support large-scale, accountable, and collaborative workflows throughout the entire lifecycle of medical guidelines.

To address these challenges, Dr. Jennifer Caswell-Jin—a practicing physician and AI healthcare researcher from the School of Medicine—has teamed up with Computer Science Professor Monica Lam, Faculty Director of the Open Virtual Lab and an expert in modular open-source platforms and deployed large-scale agentic systems. Together, we aim to tackle the following central problem:

Central Question. What breakthroughs are required for AI systems to achieve the accuracy, accountability, and collaborative capacity essential for high-stakes medical decision-making—and to empower research teams to tackle large-scale, long-term, and multi-faceted medical workflows?

Key Concepts. To unlock the true potential of AI in medicine, we need a new operating environment explicitly designed to support the complex, collaborative processes at the heart of healthcare and medical research. 

We propose AOS (Agentic Operating System)—an open, modular platform built to achieve the accuracy, accountability, and scale that current AI systems lack, especially for long-horizon, complex medical workflows. AOS stands apart not just as a collection of components, but as an integrated ecosystem that unites reusable agentic modules with a robust knowledge infrastructure. 

At the core of AOS is a novel Semantic File System—a persistent, layered knowledge substrate that provides a unified backbone for the platform (see Figure 1). This semantic file system ingests and organizes a wide array of data sources—text, PDFs, databases, images—into a richly indexed, queryable memory. Every artifact, result, and workflow step becomes a persistent, sharable object, supporting robust provenance, versioning, and full auditability across every application.

Built on this substrate are foundational interoperable and reusable neurosymbolic modules for knowledge retrieval and decision making designed for the scientific workflow. Our current suite includes SLIDERS \[11\], which transforms unstructured text into structured tables—successfully applied in gene candidate discovery and readily applicable to clinical guideline curation. Both SatIR (for constraint-aware search) and VERDICT \[12\] (for accountable decision making) utilize Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) \[13\]; although originally developed for clinical trial matching, these modules can be directly applied to guideline-based treatment recommendations. To facilitate integration and future extensibility, we will define clear and standardized API interfaces, making it straightforward to reuse, combine, and extend these modules within evolving research and clinical workflows.

The platform further supports large-scale, collaborative workflows through dedicated team workspaces and application platforms for interaction and oversight, all accessible via an intuitive user console. 

In summary, AOS integrates a persistent, auditable semantic file system with interoperable neurosymbolic retrieval and decision modules in an extensible platform, enabling developers to efficiently build and customize agents for diverse workflows. These agents make advanced, transparent AI capabilities seamlessly available to clinicians and researchers across a broad spectrum of medical research and practice tasks.

Research Plan. The development of AOS will be driven by real-world requirements from both medical research and patient care, with the breast cancer living clinical practice guideline serving as the central use case to inform requirements, guide system architecture, and shape ongoing evaluation.

Aim 1 — Core AOS Architecture Informed by Clinical Guideline Use Case: Design and implement the foundational components of AOS—including a persistent semantic file system, modular APIs, fine-grained access control, provenance tracking, and an AOS-aware coding agent—informed by the demands identified in the breast cancer guideline workflow \[14\]. This ensures that all platform capabilities are closely aligned with the realities of evidence synthesis, guideline creation, and clinical data integration. These systems will enable seamless management, interoperability, and auditability for diverse agent modules and user-generated artifacts.

Aim 2 — Guideline Reproduction and Application to Patient Records: Use AOS to reproduce and automate the literature review, evidence synthesis, and curation required for the breast cancer living guideline. Then, as part of an IRB-approved study \[14\], we will apply SMT-based modules to test whether guideline-based recommendations \[10\] can be accurately and accountably to real patient records in Oncoshare, a linked EMR/registry Stanford dataset \[15\]. This use case will both inform continued system refinement and serve as a real-world benchmark. Evaluation will focus on quantitative and qualitative improvements in accuracy, recall, extensibility, and transparency, through rigorous testing in authentic research and clinical contexts.

Aim 3 — Open-Source Release and Community Building: Release AOS and its core modules as open-source software with comprehensive documentation and example workflows. By fostering a vibrant community of users and contributors, the project will accelerate innovation, promote extensibility, and drive broad adoption of AOS in healthcare and medical science.

Conclusion. This project stands to advance both medicine and computer science in meaningful ways. Medically, its success will accelerate the development and dissemination of clinical guidelines through AI, with a concrete demonstration in the living breast cancer guidelines pioneered by the project’s co-PI. Technically, AOS establishes a new standard for medical AI by addressing challenges of accuracy, accountability, and scalability—supporting robust, transparent workflows for complex, long-horizon clinical and research needs. Its modular architecture, built on a persistent semantic file system and reusable neurosymbolic components, enables seamless creation and deployment of advanced AI solutions for tasks ranging from literature synthesis and patient record analysis to clinical trial matching, guideline recommendations, and more. As an open, extensible platform, AOS fosters innovation and collaboration, making trustworthy AI broadly practical for medical research and practice. Just as Linux became the backbone of modern computing, AOS is poised to underpin the future of reliable, open AI in healthcare.

## Transforming RNA-Seq Analysis of Cancer Drug Resistance  with a Deep Research AI Assistant {#transforming-rna-seq-analysis-of-cancer-drug-resistance-with-a-deep-research-ai-assistant}

Prof. James Ford, School of Medicine, Stanford University

Dr. Lisa McPherson, School of Medicine, Stanford University

Charlie Hempstead, Every Cure

Prof. Chieh-Ju Chao, Mayo Clinic

Monica Lam, Computer Science Department, Stanford University

**Abstract**

Cancer treatments often fail when tumors develop resistance, leaving patients with fewer options and worse outcomes. Understanding how this resistance emerges is essential for developing more effective therapies. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) can reveal genes that behave differently in resistant cancer cells, offering clues to the molecular mechanisms involved. However, turning these massive datasets into meaningful discoveries is slow and error-prone, because researchers must manually filter thousands of genes and search through scattered literature to identify a few promising targets for validation.

We propose to transform this process by creating an AI-powered deep research assistant. At its core will be an accurate, citation-linked biomedical knowledge graph that supports a literature-based question answering (QA) agent capable of retrieving and synthesizing evidence with high precision. Building on this foundation, we will develop an interactive assistant that helps researchers interpret experimental data, link findings to known resistance pathways, prioritize genes based on biological plausibility, and design follow-up experiments with fully cited evidence. We will also explore an early autonomous version of the assistant that attempts this analysis on its own. This work will not only accelerate the discovery of drug resistance mechanisms, but also drive advances in AI research assistants and lay essential groundwork for achieving fully automated deep scientific research.

**Background.** Resistance to chemotherapy is a central challenge in oncology \[1\]. Tumors often acquire mutations that alter drug targets or activate compensatory pathways, allowing growth despite treatment. This causes treatment failure, shorter progression-free and overall survival, higher costs, and reduced quality of life. Overcoming resistance requires understanding the molecular mechanisms driving it.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) can reveal differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between resistant and sensitive cancer cells, offering clues to resistance mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets. But turning statistical DEG lists into actionable biological insights requires deep expertise and exhaustive literature analysis. Researchers must narrow thousands of candidates to a few for validation as experimental validation (e.g., qPCR, functional assays) is labor- and resource-intensive. The current bottleneck is the literature-driven prioritization step: synthesizing complex results with scattered knowledge is slow and error-prone, with researchers easily missing potentially relevant literature across disparate fields \[2\], limiting the impact of RNA-seq studies on clinical progress.

**Core Idea.** We propose a large language model (LLM)-based **deep research assistant** to accelerate RNA-seq analysis, focusing on resistance to the PARP inhibitor Talazoparib. It will support:

* **Functional enrichment interpretation**—assessing biological relevance to cancer resistance.

* **Hypothesis generation**—linking DEGs to known resistance pathways.

* **DEG refinement**—prioritizing candidates by biological plausibility, not just fold change.

* **Contextualization**—cross-checking findings against prior reports in similar cancer-drug settings.

* **Validation design**—suggesting follow-up experiments (qPCR, knockouts, functional assays).

* **Narrative support**—helping craft the discussion and significance sections for publications.

Two key challenges drive this work: (i) performing **exhaustive literature search**, and (ii) **synthesizing experimental data with literature evidence** to produce actionable findings.

**Central Hypothesis:** An LLM-powered deep research assistant can enhance PARP inhibitor Talazoparib resistance research workflows by autonomously integrating literature knowledge extraction, RNA-seq data analysis, and experimental design recommendations through a unified knowledge graph framework to elucidate mechanisms of therapeutic resistance.  

**Approach. *Exhaustive literature search.*** Current methods fail: direct LLM prompting and embedding- based RAG approaches (e.g., **ChatGPT** and **Perplexity**) have unacceptably high false-negative rates for deep technical questions \[3\]. We will develop a literature-based biomedical question answering system with enhanced accuracy through a citation-linked knowledge graph that achieves superior precision and recall compared to existing resources. Our pipeline performs: (1) entity extraction to identify typed biomedical entities (genes, drugs, pathwaysncer types), (2) canonicalization via entity linking to identifiers (UMLS), (3) relation extraction to connect entities into triples, (4) consistency checks to flag type mismatches, duplicates, and schema violations. Modules will leverage external knowledge sources for disambiguation and enrichment. Early results on random PubMed samples show a \> 90% accuracy rate, far above typical biomedical KGs \[4\], which also lack source citations.

***Synthesizing data with literature.*** While systems like **STORM** \[5\] (developed by the OVAL Lab, Prof. Lam) have pioneered literature assistants—attracting 800,000+ users and generating 1.3M+ articles—and inspired tools such as **ChatGPT Deep Research** \[6\] and **Perplexity Pro Search** \[7\]; they are primarily general-purpose summarizers, not “deep researchers.” They cannot synthesize experimental results with exhaustive literature search to design the next experiments. For example, a modest fold-change in a known signaling hub may be more biologically important than a large change in an uncharacterized gene.

**Stage 1 – Interactive Assistant.** We will create an interactive system that answers natural-language questions about experimental data, performs exhaustive literature searchesptures expert prioritization heuristics, suggests validation experiments, and generates structured draft reports.

**Stage 2 – Autonomous Agent.** Building on Stage 1, we will develop an agent that autonomously performs literature queries and data synthesis using LLM-native or expert-derived heuristics, by extending our mindmap approach into a systematic framework for organizing research findings \[8\]. Comparing agent results with interactive assistant outcomes will identify key challenges for fully automated research.

**Innovation.** This will be the first high-accuracy, citation-linked biomedical KG integrated into an interactive deep research assistant for RNA-seq analysis—and the first deployment of such a system in an end-to-end laboratory research workflow.

**Research Plan. Aim 1 – Build an accurate KG extractor.** We will develop a knowledge graph (KG) extraction pipeline and evaluate its precision and recall against BioRED \[4\], a state-of-the-art biomedical KG benchmark. Medical doctors will help analyze errors and guide iterative refinement. We expect to achieve higher precision and recall than current methods. Clinicians at Stanford University, Mayo Clinic, and EveryCure, a nonprofit focusing on repurposing drugs for rare diseases, will collaborate to design a comprehensive KG schema that supports diverse medical research projects. Using secured computational resources from the NAIRR pilot, we will apply the pipeline to over one million PubMed articles, and medical professionals will evaluate extractions from a cardiology subset to measure precision and recall.

**Aim 2 – Create an effective literature-based QA agent.** We will build a question-answering agent that translates natural-language questions into Cypher queries, retrieves source-linked evidence from the KG stored in Neo4j, and uses a carefully tuned RAG layer to generate answers with minimal hallucination. A cancer drug resistance researcher will prepare a set of real research questions, attempt to answer them via a manual literature search for one day, and then repeat the task using our QA agent. We expect the agent to help the researcher uncover significantly more relevant findings in far less time. To show generality,  we will replicate this evaluation with EveryCure researchers in repurposing drugs for rare diseases.

**Aim 3 – Prototype an interactive deep research assistant.** We will develop an interactive assistant using RNA-seq analysis as the test case. The assistant will capture users’ prioritization heuristics, organize all evidence with full citations, and draft complete reports. Researchers will evaluate its usefulness, completeness, and time savings compared to current workflows.

**Aim 4 – Experiment with an autonomous deep researcher.** We will build a prototype autonomous agent that attempts full RNA-seq analysis using either LLM-native or researcher-supplied prioritization heuristics. We will compare its results to those produced by a human using the interactive assistant to assess performance gaps and technical challenges, providing insights needed to advance toward fully automated deep research.

**Impact and Conclusion.** This system will accelerate discovery of resistance mechanisms to Talazoparib and other cancer drugs by enabling researchers to focus validation efforts on the most literature-informed targets. Because RNA-seq is widely used, the approach could generalize to many other areas of biomedical research. The project will also produce an accurate, citation-linked biomedical knowledge graph that can be reused for other scientific applications. This work will not only transform RNA-seq analysis and accelerate cancer resistance research, but also drive advances in AI research assistants and lay essential groundwork for achieving fully automated deep scientific research.

Charlie Hempstead, VP of Platform, Every Cure, provides expertise on drug discovery for rare diseases. Chieh-Ju Chao, MD (Mayo Clinic) is an Assistant Professor of Medicine in Cardiovascular Medicine, providing medical domain expertise to ensure the accuracy and generalizability of the knowledge graph across biomedical fields. Monica Lam, PhD (Stanford Computer Science) is the Kleiner Perkins, Mayfield, Sequoia Capital Professor and Faculty Director of the Open Virtual Assistant Lab, with expertise in knowledge graph construction and LLM-based research systems.
