Abstract

Systematic reviews requires comprehensive evidence synthesis from large docu-
ment corpora to answer targeted research questions and are foundational in finance,
social sciences, and other technical fields. Manual construction of evidence ta-
bles is labor-intensive, and recent LLM-based assistants relying on embedding or
keyword search often fail to meet the coverage standards of systematic reviews.
We introduce SLIDERS, a novel LLM-based methodology for automatically as-
sembling evidence tables tailored to research questions for systematic reviews.
SLIDERS extracts full-text excerpts that serve as direct evidence or as provenance
for structured data. Core to SLIDERS is an autonomous evidence reconciliation
coding agent that iteratively writes SQL to analyze and reconcile extracted ev-
idence, bringing together information fragmented across documents, resolving
inconsistencies and removing duplicates across records. We evaluate SLIDERS on
BrowseComp+, achieving 90% accuracy, and show that it outperforms all baselines
on additional benchmarks within the LLM’s context window. To assess scalability,
we introduce two new evidence-synthesis benchmarks, WikiCeleb100 (3.9M to-
kens) and FinQ100 (36M tokens), which include follow-up questions beyond the
original research query. Although SLIDERS synthesizes evidence knowing only
the original question, it answers 78.9% and 55.2% of all questions correctly on
WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100, respectively.

Introduction

Systematic reviews are foundational to evidence-based research in domains such as medicine, social
sciences, policy, finance, and law \[1–5\]. By rigorously synthesizing evidence to answer targeted
research questions, they inform scientists and decision-makers in research, policy, and practice.
However, conducting a high-quality systematic review is a complex, resource-intensive undertaking.
The process involves identifying eligible studies, extracting and standardizing relevant data, and
integrating findings into a coherent synthesis, while adhering to transparent reporting standards \[6–8\].
Each stage requires substantial expert effort. A recent analysis indicates a mean completion time of
over a year (67.3 weeks) per review \[9\]. Even after narrowing down eligible studies, reviewers must
manually extract data from numerous sources, with domain specific averages ranging from 20 to
72 documents \[10\], making the process highly labor-intensive and time-consuming \[11, 12\]. This
investment of time and effort represents a major barrier to timely and scalable knowledge synthesis.

We address the challenge of automating information extraction from scientific documents after
they have been identified as relevant. LLMs hold considerable promise for accelerating evidence
synthesis \[13, 12\], but their use poses important challenges. First, when processing lengthy documents
or extensive contexts, LLMs may miss or inadequately extract relevant information, leading to
incomplete or inaccurate results \[14–16\]. Second, the combined length of all documents considered
in a systematic review often exceeds the LLM’s context window, making it impractical to analyze
all texts simultaneously in a single prompt \[17, 18\]. Overcoming these limitations is crucial to fully
realizing the potential of LLMs for large-scale, automated evidence synthesis.

This paper examines a key question for automating evidence synthesis: How can we accurately
extract all relevant evidence from a document collection that may exceed an LLM’s context window
and answer questions in systematic reviews? One common approach is RAG, which selects passages
relevant to a given question \[19–21\]. However, this selective retrieval can omit evidence necessary
for a comprehensive synthesis. While retrieving more passages can increase coverage, it also further
constrains the available context length. Alternatively, chunk-based extraction processes documents in
segments and collects structured records \[22–24\]. For instance, DocETL extracts records according
to user-defined schemas \[23\]. Yet as the number of extracted records grows, their combined length
may also exceed the context window, rendering simple concatenation infeasible for large collections.

First contribution: SLIDERS evidence synthesis agent. This paper introduces SLIDERS, a
fully automated, scalable, and database-centric agent for evidence synthesis from large document
collections. Similar to previous chunk-based extraction methods, SLIDERS extracts information
chunk by chunk and stores it in a database. However, SLIDERS introduces several key innovations.
First, it automatically determines the database schema, eliminating the need for manual design.
Recognizing that users commonly refine their queries or pose follow-up questions as they interact
with results, SLIDERS proactively designs the schema to accommodate a broad range of related and
subsequent inquiries, greatly enhancing flexibility. This allows the cost of extraction be amortized
across the different queries. In addition, to mitigate issues associated with out-of-context analysis,
SLIDERS carefully constructs the context for each chunk.

Second, and more importantly, empirical findings indicate that chunk-based extraction will inevitably
produce a very “dirty” database, one in which the extracted information is often redundant, contradic-
tory, or incomplete, all of which must be reconciled for comprehensive synthesis. To address this
challenge, SLIDERS incorporates an autonomous coding agent that systematically reconciles and
curates the database.

The resulting clean, reconciled database serves as a powerful foundation for SLIDERS’ third key in-
novation: flexible and dynamic exploration of evidence. By integrating LLM language understanding
with the power of database queries, SLIDERS can answer not only the original review questions but
also a wide range of follow-up inquiries without the need for repeated extraction. In sum, SLIDERS
fully leverages the scalability and versatility of databases to efficiently store, curate, and answer
questions across arbitrarily large collections of long documents.

Database cleaning and reconciliation is a longstanding area of research, encompassing various
approaches for error detection and repair\[25\]. This includes traditional methods rooted in statistical
regularities and integrity constraints\[26, 27\], as well as more recent LLM-based techniques designed
to identify typos and outliers at the column level\[28\], or approaches leveraging labeled data to surface
underlying data quality issues\[29, 30\]. However, these methods often focus narrowly on resolving

Effective reconciliation relies on leveraging the full text, just as LLMs do when documents fit within

the source text spans and reasoning for each extracted value. This design ensures that sources remains
accessible for reconciliation, even when the document collection exceeds the LLM’s context window.

Second contribution: An effective autonomous database reconciliation coding agent.

Despite the sophistication of today’s LLMs, instructing them to directly reconcile databases is not
viable. For example, LLMs would attempt to ingest the entire database in their context, which does
not scale, and their justifications for resolving conflicting data can diverge from users’ objectives.

To address these challenges, we have developed a robust reconciliation strategy encoded directly in
the agent’s prompt. At scale, the agent issues tailored SQL commands for each database table, first
identifying reconciliation issues and then resolving them based on observed data. The process begins
with grouping records by primary keys, consolidating related facts from different sections of a single
document or across multiple documents. The agent’s instructions specify strategies for addressing
a range of error types, including duplications, inconsistencies, and partial or complementary infor-
mation, using detailed analysis of supporting text spans and extraction rationales. Additionally, the
prompt outlines resolution priorities, such as preferring values derived from direct, reliable quotations
over those that are computed. Crucially, the reconciliation code adapts dynamically to the structure
and anomalies of the data, enabling context-aware and scalable curation.

To ensure the fidelity of edits, the agent is further instructed to verify its changes by comparing
the proposed database updates against the intended update. If modifications deviate, they are
automatically reverted and the agent receives feedback for refinement. This iterative loop enables
robust, automated, and context-sensitive database reconciliation without human supervision.

Third contribution: a large-scale evidence management dataset. In real-world applications,
systematic reviews are often conducted on datasets that far exceed the context windows of current
LLMs. Yet, to our knowledge, there are no existing datasets of this scale, and none that support multi-
turn interactions or follow-up questioning. This paper introduces two new benchmarks: WikiCeleb100
and FinQ100, each comprising 100 documents drawn from Wikipedia and 10-Q financial filings,
respectively. We further evaluate SLIDERS on BrowseComp+ \[31\], a deep-research benchmark, as
well as on other document QA datasets that fit within an LLM’s context window. Our results show
that SLIDERS outperforms the strongest baselines by 19 and 27 percentage points on WikiCeleb100
and FinQ100, respectively. Notably, SLIDERS also achieves superior performance on tasks where
the input documents fit entirely within the LLM’s context window.

Chunking Methods. RAG grounds LLM outputs by retrieving chunks from an external corpus

processing workflows \[23\], domain-specific extraction systems \[57, 58\], and hierarchical or hybrid
retrieval methods \[51, 59\]. While these approaches scale for extraction, when the number of evidences
grows, they are also constrained by LLM’s context window. SLIDERS instead keeps chunk-level
extractions in database and performs database reconciliation using the coding agent.

3
The SLIDERS Method

SLIDERS constructs a database of document-grounded evidence and autonomously reconciles incon-
sistencies through iterative investigation. The framework proceeds as follows: First, it automatically
generates evidence variables and extracts structured records from document chunks. Next, a recon-
ciliation agent queries the database, examines supporting source passages, and writes custom code
to systematically group related records and resolve redundancy, contradictions, or incompleteness.
Finally, a response synthesis agent leverages the curated database to answer both the initial research
question and an array of related follow-up inquiries.

Task Formulation. Given a research question q and a set of eligible documents D, the objective is to
construct a reusable evidence database Zq that enables robust answers to the research question using
the curated evidence. The evidence database may contain multiple evidence tables. Each table is
structured with columns corresponding to question-relevant evidence variables and rows representing
document-backed evidence records; each record is linked to its source document, supporting text span,
and provenance metadata. The evidence variables may capture typed values (e.g., dates, categories,
measurements) as well as free-text fields (e.g., rationales, or descriptions). Crucially, the database is
designed to be reusable across related research questions. At answer time, a coding agent dynamically
reorganizes the evidence to produce the precise view required to address the current question.

3.1
Automated Generation of Evidence Variables

A central innovation of SLIDERS is its ability to automatically generate a tailored set of evidence
variables directly from the research question and a representative sample of the document metadata.

Definition 1. The Evidence Variable Generation Task takes a question q and document metadata M,
and returns a set of evidence variables for the evidence table:
Vq,M = {v1, . . . , vn},
where each v = ⟨vn, d, τ, λ, ι⟩.
Here, vn is the evidence variable name; d is a semantic description; τ is the data type; λ specifies the
normalization rule; and ι is the extraction instruction for the variable.

All evidence variables are strongly typed, enabling support for precise selection, aggregation, fil-
tering, and comparison during synthesis. Normalization rules, such as currency conversion or date
formatting, are included to ensure consistency and comparability between independently extracted
excerpts. Furthermore, each variable is recorded alongside its supporting source text, rationale, and
detailed provenance, ensuring that all synthesized answers can be directly traced back to the original
document language. This design enables transparent, document-grounded synthesis and interactive
re-exploration of the evidence database for subsequent queries.

We instruct the LLM to extract not only variables relevant to the immediate research question, but
also broader latent attributes that can support future or follow-up queries. This results in a flexible
evidence schema that serves both current and anticipated information needs.

We determine empirically that when a chunk contains no relevant excerpt for an evidence variable,
an in-context extractor may hallucinate a plausible but unsupported value because the output format
forces the model to conform to the schema \[60, 61\]. To maintain high precision, we introduce
a relevance gate. Before extraction, the model first decides whether the chunk contains relevant
evidence that can instantiate at least one generated evidence variable. Extraction is invoked only
when the gate passes; otherwise the chunk contributes no excerpts to the evidence table.

The extraction task over the document set can be parallelized, as it operates independently on each
chunk. For details on our technique for constructing metadata that enriches context and improves
extraction quality, please refer to Appendix A.2.

3.3
Autonomous Evidence Reconciliation

Once the initial evidence table has been assembled through chunk-based extraction, it inevitably
contains a high-recall but “dirty” collection of document-grounded records. Entries are often re-
dundant, fragmented, or even contradictory, and information relevant to a single question may be
scattered across multiple segments \[62, 63\]. Moreover, as the scale of the document set grows, the
raw evidence table can far exceed the context capacity of large language models.

Definition 3. The Reconciliation Task takes a question q, evidence variables Vq,M, and a raw
evidence table Zq produced by chunk-level extraction, and returns a reconciled evidence table:

Discovering reconciliation tasks.
We aim to develop an agent that operates with full autonomy,
proactively identifying reconciliation needs by querying the database, rather than relying on pre-
defined templates. To accurately identify issues, the agent must examine both the values and their
corresponding source documents, especially when disambiguating between similar extractions.

However, when a LLM agent is granted access to the database, two common pitfalls emerge. First,
the agent may only inspect the initial subset of rows (e.g., the first 10) and attempt to generalize
issues from this limited sample. This approach fails because each extracted value may originate from
a different source, and naïve generalization can lead to systematic errors (e.g., always selecting the
maximum revenue, which may not be appropriate in every case). Second, the agent may attempt to

Since the agent must consider each value alongside its provenance and extraction rationale, there
is a need for strategies that allow the LLM to process the data in manageable segments. This
requires grouping of data by bringing related information together, enabling effective discovery of
reconciliation tasks without requiring comparisons across all rows simultaneously (Figure 3).

Constructing evidence groups.
To inspect related records together, the agent identifies a set of
grouping variables and determines canonical (standardized) representations for their values. For
each reconciliation task, these grouping variables are chosen based on the original question, and the
evidence table. For example, in financial analysis, grouping might be done by company and fiscal
year to bring together all records relevant to an annual report. In a clinical review, records might be
grouped according to intervention, population, outcome, and time point, so that all instances referring
to the same clinical comparison can be analyzed jointly.

For each record, the agent generates a grouping key by converting each relevant variable to its
canonical form, ensuring that different surface expressions (such as name variations or synonyms)
are treated consistently. Records that share the same canonical grouping key are collected into one
group, allowing the agent to efficiently reconcile related data points within each group.

Diagnosing records from source context.
Within a group, the agent reviews the extracted values
along with their associated source spans and rationales. We ask the model to analyze and diagnose
one of the four reconciliation issues. First, there may be duplicate records that represent the same
underlying finding; in such cases, these can be consolidated while retaining all relevant supporting
sources. Second, the group may include complementary records that address different parts of the
question, and these can be combined or aggregated for a more comprehensive answer. Third, there
may be conflicting records that present genuinely different values for the same fields, requiring the
agent to determine which value should be retained. Finally, apparent conflicts may arise due to
varying assumptions or levels of granularity; in these cases, all records should be preserved.

Writing Custom Reconciliation Code.
The agent generates SQL code to address the issues
identified during the diagnosis stage. We observed that, when prompted to fix problems by providing
only a description of the issue to the LLM, its reasoning often diverged from the actual problem.
For instance, the agent might select the earliest date to distinguish between two records without
considering provenance or rationale, or it might choose computed values over those explicitly stated.

To mitigate these issues, we provide the coding agent with guidelines for each issue type (see
Appendix F.1.3). This guidance enables accurate reconciliation across a range of scenarios, such as
resolving conflicting field values, eliminating duplicates, or merging complementary records.

3.4
Synthesizing Answers and Reusing Evidence

Once the reconciled evidence table has been constructed, SLIDERS generates the response from
the reconciled evidence table. Importantly, the reconciled table may still be very large, so the agent
uses evidence variables to retrieve a evidence context for final synthesis. The final answer is then
generated by a series of inspections of the selected evidence context, with citations to the supporting
spans. More importantly, this evidence table can be used for answering further questions.

4
Evaluation on Benchmarks Beyond LLM Context Limits

We evaluate SLIDERS’s ability to synthesize evidences from documents. In all tasks, the system is
given a research question and a document corpus with the goal of answering the question.

We first evaluate on BrowseComp+ \[31\], a deep-research benchmark which tests whether systems
can identify and synthesize evidence distributed across multiple documents. To test the follow-up
analysis of SLIDERS, we introduce WikiCeleb100 (WC), a new corpus of 100 Wikipedia articles
with a research question and 21 related follow-up questions, designed to test reusable evidence-table
construction and subgroup comparison. WC comprises Wikipedia articles for the 100 most-viewed
celebrity pages from November 2025 to January 2026 (3.9M tokens); the research question is “Who
debuted at the youngest age across the following industries: Music, Film, Content Creation, and
Other?”. It requires extracting each celebrity’s date of birth, debut date, and industry from across all
100 articles. As a larger follow-up analysis benchmark, we present FinQ100 (FQ). FQ comprises
the most recent 10-Q filings of 100 randomly selected SEC-listed companies (36M tokens) with 25
questions. The research question is “Which company has the lowest long-term borrowing?”. This
question is harder because many filings do not state borrowing explicitly, requiring inference from
context. Full descriptions of the benchmarks, construction protocols, and metrics are in Appendix B.

Metrics. All result tables report the accuracy of the final answer against the gold answer. Browse-
Comp+, Loong and Oolong use their official metrics. For FinanceBench, WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100,
a GPT-4.1 judge compares it with the gold answer, giving partial credit on WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100
as in Oolong. It agrees with human labels at Cohen’s κ = 0.758 (Appendix B.4).

Baselines.
We compare against retrieval, base-LM, and agentic baselines; full implementation
details are in Appendix C. We use RAG \[19\], LongRAG \[20\], GraphRAG \[21\] with Qwen3-4B
for indexing and GPT-4.1 as the reader. We use DocETL \[23\] and use the same evidence variables
as ours with GPT-4.1. We use RLM \[38\], following the original RLM paper, we run RLM with
GPT-5 / GPT-5-mini. For RLM, we give all questions of WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 at once, so
that it can build a shared state in its Python REPL and reuse it across questions. GraphRAG builds
its graph index once, before seeing any question, and reuses it for every question. For SLIDERS,
GPT-4.1-mini handles evidence-variable-aware extraction and GPT-4.1 handles evidence-variable
generation, reconciliation and final synthesis. We also report using Qwen3.5-122B-A10B.

Observation 1: SLIDERS improves evidence synthesis as evidence becomes longer and more
distributed. BrowseComp+ evaluates whether a system can identify and synthesize evidence
distributed across multiple documents for a single deep-research question. On this benchmark,
SLIDERS achieves 90.00, outperforming the strongest non-SLIDERS baseline, RLM, by 10 points,
as shown in Table 1. Retrieval-based methods such as RAG and LongRAG perform substantially
worse, suggesting that answering these questions requires more than selecting a small set of relevant
passages. On average, RAG and LongRAG only retrieved 10.4% and 12.5% of relevant documents
out of all the relevant documents in the corpus. Moreover, the RAG and LongRAG retrievers return
zero evidence docs for 57% and 49% questions, respectively. In contrast, SLIDERS extracts the
relevant evidence variables, groups related records, reconciles overlapping or conflicting evidence,
and then answers from the resulting evidence table.

5
Evaluation on Benchmarks Within LLM Context Limits

After demonstrating SLIDERS’s advantages on large-scale datasets that exceed LLM context windows,
we next assess its performance on benchmarks where the entire evidence set fits within those limits.
First, we evaluate on MedEvidence \[64\], a systematic-review benchmark where questions are drawn
from the conclusions of 100 systematic reviews. Here, documents are small (upto 62k tokens),
so this task does not fully leverage SLIDERS’s large-scale extraction and reconciliation strengths.
Nevertheless, SLIDERS achieves a score of 71.5, outperforming one-shot GPT-5, which scores 66.2.

To broaden the comparison with techniques constrained by LLM context windows, we also evaluate
SLIDERS on three additional “long-context QA” benchmarks: FinanceBench (FB)\[65\], a single-
document financial QA benchmark with 150 questions over public filings (up to 363K tokens);
Loong\[66\], a multi-document QA benchmark requiring every provided document per instance (∼11
documents, up to 300K tokens, across finance, law, and academic papers, in both English and
Chinese); and Oolong \[16\] (Oolong-Synth, 256K tokens), an aggregation benchmark requiring
models to classify individual data points and aggregate them into a global answer.

Baselines. In addition to baselines in Sec. 4, we also evaluate: Base LM: GPT-4.1 and Qwen3.5-
122B-A10B \[67\] (1M-token context), CoA \[22\]: sequential chunk summarization with running
context (GPT-4.1-mini / GPT-4.1), and RLM with GPT 4.1 and GPT 4.1-mini as LLM backbone.

Observation 1: Using Evidence Table also improves long context QA accuracy, even when the
corpus already fits in the model’s context window. As shown in Table 3, SLIDERS achieves
the best average performance on the three within-window benchmarks, outperforming the strongest
baseline (GPT-4.1) by 9.54 points on average. The largest within-window gain occurs on Oolong,
where every question requires aggregating many local reasonings into a global answer – the regime
in which each excerpt is important or they are widely scattered through the corpus. That the gain
appears even when the input fits in the base model’s window indicates that accuracy is bounded by
whether the retained context supports targeted retrieval, not by raw context length. SLIDERS with
Qwen3.5-122B-A10B is close to its GPT-4.1 version and beats base LMs on Loong and Oolong.

Observation 2: Each stage of SLIDERS contributes to robust evidence synthesis. Figure 6(a)
shows that removing major components generally reduces performance across FinanceBench, Loong,
and Oolong. The full system achieves the best result on FinanceBench and Oolong, with especially
large gains on Oolong: removing chunk-level extraction drops performance from 64.67 to 40.00,
showing that systematic evidence extraction is necessary when relevant information is distributed
across long inputs. Removing reconciliation also lowers performance on all three benchmarks,
indicating that extracted evidence must still be organized and resolved before answer generation. The
only exception is Loong, where the Direct Table variant slightly exceeds the full system by 0.08
points. The extracted table was already small, additional reconciliation may provide less benefit.

Observation 3: SLIDERS is more stable than baselines as token length increases. Figure 6(b)
compares accuracy across token lengths on Loong and FinanceBench. On Loong, SLIDERS remains
above 70% even at the longest inputs, while RLM drops sharply as the context grows, reaching below
50% at the largest token length. A similar pattern appears on FinanceBench. SLIDERS maintains
high accuracy across longer contexts and remains strongest at the largest input size. These results
suggest that SLIDERS does not depend on directly reasoning over the entire long input.

6
Analysis and Discussions

How much reconciliation is needed?
Here we evaluate the effect of the reconciliation agent
in removing duplicates and inconsistencies, and consolidating incomplete records after extraction.
As shown in Table 11 (Appendix), reconciliation yields the largest compression on WikiCeleb100
and FinQ100, with reductions of 1.98× and 4.51×, respectively, consistent with the measure of
improvement due to reconciliation reported in Table 2.

What kind of reconciliation is needed? Figure 7 reports the breakdown of reconciliation issues
identified by SLIDERS. Records requiring deduplication, consolidation, and resolving conflicts.
Financial and legal corpora are dominated by conflict resolution, while WikiCeleb100 requires more
consolidation because evidence about the same entity is distributed across sections.

How good is automated reconciliation? We manually compile the reconciled evidence table
for WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 (Appendix D.2) and use it to compare reconciliatin accuracy of
SLIDERS.Reconciliation recovers 92.5% and 90.1% of the gold rows, with a structural F1 of 0.91 on
WikiCeleb100. On FinQ100, precision is 0.49 because the agent keeps multiple rows for 16 entities it
failed to canonicalize. When the correct value is among the extracted candidates, the agent selects
it 80% (WikiCeleb100) and 81% (FinQ100) of the time, and most wrong values occur because the
correct record was not retained.

Failure Analysis. In Fig. 8(a), on the Chinese classification subset, documents are usually under
16K tokens and the task is label prediction rather than synthesis over distributed evidence, so the
benefits of using evidence variables, extraction, and reconciliation are limited. In Fig. 8(b), we
observe that performance is lower for questions that require deep financial domain understanding.
Finally, exact counting and numerical questions remain challenging because they require not only
preserving evidence, but also correctly classifying borderline cases (Fig. 8(c)), deduplicating records,
normalizing values, and aggregating them. This limitation is especially visible in FinQ100, where
many failures require precise numerical reasoning over financial evidence. On BrowseComp+, 8 of
the 15 failures arise from incorrect reasoning over the final table, while 5 occur because SLIDERS
does did not synthesize the correct evidence table.

7
Conclusion

We introduced SLIDERS, a scalable, database-centric agent for automated evidence extraction and
synthesis across large document collections. By combining context-aware chunk-based extraction, au-
tonomous database reconciliation, and source-grounded evidence management, SLIDERS addresses
key limitations of existing LLM-based approaches while supporting flexible follow-up queries. Ex-
periments across multiple benchmarks demonstrate substantial improvements over strong baselines,
including gains of 19 and 27 percentage points on WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100, respectively. These
results highlight the potential of database-centric agents to enable more accurate, scalable, and flexible
evidence synthesis for systematic reviews.

A.1
Question Decomposition

The original question posed by the user is not directly suitable for all stages of the pipeline. In
particular, extraction and reconciliation are intermediate steps that impose different requirements on
task formulation. Extraction should enumerate all relevant candidate evidence from each document
chunk, while reconciliation should combine these candidates into a globally consistent database.
Conditioning either stage on the original question can cause them to prematurely answer the question
rather than constructing the intermediate representation.

Consider the query: “Return the second poem about the Great Wall of China.” Suppose the first chunk
contains one relevant poem (P1), and the second chunk contains two relevant poems (P2, P3). If the
extractor is conditioned on the original question, it may incorrectly interpret the ordinal constraint
“second” at the chunk level: it extracts nothing from the first chunk (since no second poem exists
locally) and extracts P3 from the second chunk (as the second poem within that chunk). This results
in an incorrect global outcome, since the true second poem across the entire document collection is
P2. Similar issues arise for other global constraints such as superlatives, rankings, and aggregations.

To prevent such premature answer-oriented behavior, SLIDERS decomposes the input question q
into component-specific queries. We generate an extraction query qe that specifies what information
should be identified from each document chunk, and a reconciliation query qr that defines how partial
extractions should be combined and cleaned to form a coherent database. Schema induction and final
answer synthesis, in contrast, operate directly on the original question q, as these stages are explicitly
responsible for capturing the full semantic intent of the user’s request and producing the final answer.

A.2
Context-Aware Chunking

Each chunk retains the raw text as well as its structural metadata, including the current heading
path (e.g., Header 1 →Header 1.1 →Header 1.1.3), the chunk index, the document title, and the
document description. This ensures that every chunk is self-contained, carrying both global and local
context needed for faithful extraction.

B.2

As shown in Table 4, the benchmark suite covers a range of document scales, data sources, and

B.3
Benchmark Construction

Question construction begins from a seed query: for WikiCeleb100, “Which artist made their debut
at the youngest age across Film, Music, Content Creation, and Other industries?”; and for FinQ100,
“Which company has the lowest long-term borrowing?” We derive follow-up questions by reusing
and recombining the extracted information, including temporal cohorts (e.g., artists debuting in the
1950s or born in the 1970s) and aggregate financial properties (e.g., companies with no long-term
borrowing or borrowing exceeding $80M).

To annotate gold answers, we use evidence extraction task to extract an initial structured representation,
then manually verify all extracted values against the original Wikipedia articles and financial filings.
Automated extraction serves only as a starting point to accelerate the annotation process; the final
ground-truth was determined entirely through manual verification and correction. We then manually
reconcile the resulting tables to obtain a consolidated database representation of each document set,
and manually author SQL queries for each question. For each benchmark, we additionally retain five
questions that cannot be answered using SQL alone.

WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 are primarily follow-up analysis benchmarks: they test whether an
evidence table built for an initial research question can support related downstream questions without
reprocessing the full corpus. They also function as large-corpus QA benchmarks, but their main
purpose is to evaluate reusable evidence-table construction.

We acknowledge that these benchmarks favor systems capable of evidence extraction and reuse.
That is intentional: this is the capability being evaluated. However, the evaluation does not give
SLIDERS privileged access to the gold table, and competing systems are free to extract, aggregate,
or build intermediate representations. The fact that removing reconciliation substantially degrades
performance on both WikiCeleb100 and FinQ100 shows that the task is not solved by extraction
alone; large-scale grouping, deduplication, conflict handling, and answer-time use of the table are
also required.

Evaluation. SLIDERS currently assumes that follow-up questions are answerable from the evidence
variables induced for the original research question. If a follow-up requires a missing variable, the
current implementation may fail. A natural extension is to detect missing variables, regenerate or
extend the schema, and run incremental extraction only for the newly required fields.

C.1
Baseline Implementations

We compare against three families of baselines: retrieval-augmented methods, base LMs operating
directly on the full corpus, and agentic scaffolding methods. All baselines use the same evaluation
protocol (LLM-as-a-judge with partial scoring) as SLIDERS. Models referred to below are listed in
Table 1 and Table 3 of the main paper.

RAG (dense retrieval).
Standard chunk-level dense retrieval. Documents are split into fixed-size
chunks of 4k tokens and indexed with Qwen3-4B as the embedding model. At inference, the question
is embedded and the top-k chunks are retrieved by cosine similarity and concatenated as context for
the GPT-4.1 reader, which generates the answer.

LongRAG.
Follows the original LongRAG recipe \[20\]: longer chunks (16k tokens) are used so that
fewer retrievals are needed and each retrieved unit carries more local context. Embedder Qwen3-4B;
reader GPT-4.1.

GraphRAG.
Microsoft’s graph-structured RAG \[21\]. The corpus is processed through entity-
relation extraction to build a knowledge graph, then community summaries are produced bottom-up.
At query time, the system selects communities relevant to the question and uses their summaries as
context. Embedder Qwen3-4B; reader GPT-4.1.

Base LMs.
We pass the full corpus directly to the model in a single prompt. We test GPT-4.1
(1M-token context) and Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (1M-token context). No retrieval, summarization, or
chunking is performed.

DocETL.
Map-reduce extraction with per-question schemas \[23\]. The system generates a plan
that maps over chunks (16k tokens) to extract structured fields, then reduces by aggregating across
chunks. Worker and reasoning model: GPT-4.1.

Chain of Agents (CoA).
Sequential chunk-by-chunk (16k tokens) summarization with a running-
context buffer \[22\]. Each agent ingests one chunk plus the running summary from the previous agent
and produces an updated summary; a final manager agent answers the question from the last summary.
Worker model: GPT-4.1-mini; reasoning model: GPT-4.1 (manager).

RLM (Recursive Language Models).
Recursive task decomposition \[38\]. The reasoning LM
(GPT-5) decides at each step whether to read a sub-document directly or to delegate to a sub-call (its
own recursive instance) on a smaller portion of the corpus; sub-calls return summarized findings to
the parent. Worker model: GPT-5-mini. We use the authors’ default recursion-depth and step-budget
settings. We run RLM for maximum 30 iterations.

D.3
Schema Induction Robustness

A natural concern is whether SLIDERS is sensitive to the choice of schema-induction model. We
generate schemas with three models of varying capability (GPT-4.1-mini, GPT-4.1, GPT-5) on Loong
and FinanceBench. The resulting schemas differ substantially: GPT-4.1 averages 1.0 tables and 3.3
fields per question vs. GPT-5 with 1.54 tables and 13.3 fields, a 4× complexity gap. Yet downstream
accuracy remains stable (Table 9): Loong average accuracy spans just 2.1 points across all three
models, and FinanceBench stays within 3.3 points, demonstrating that schema induction is not a
fragile bottleneck. One caveat is that more complex schemas can increase reconciliation difficulty,
post-reconciliation ranges widen modestly on Loong, suggesting diminishing returns from overly
detailed schemas.

D.4
Relevance Gate Analysis

We evaluate the relevance gate on 20 sampled incorrect predictions across benchmarks. Across 516
chunks, 282 were rejected by the relevance gate, with only 1 false negative, yielding a false-negative
rate of 0.4%. We note that this estimate is derived from a limited sample of pipeline-level failures
and may not be fully representative of the gate’s overall accuracy across the corpora and leave it for
future analysis. This indicates that the relevance gate is not a major source of error; most failures
arise downstream (e.g., schema mismatch or reconciliation).

D.5
Multi-Document Summarization Case Study

To demonstrate generality beyond QA, we ran SLIDERS with the prompt “Summarise the given
research papers” on three ML papers from the Loong benchmark. The system induced a 5-table
schema: PaperSummary (title, authors, abstract), PaperContributions, PaperMethods, PaperFindings,
and PaperConclusions, each with paper_title as linking key and free-text fields. It extracted 117
records across 3 papers, then aggregated them into coherent per-paper summaries. Because extraction
runs per-chunk rather than over the full corpus, it avoids information loss from context-window
pressure seen in direct LLM baselines. This case study suggests that SLIDERS may extend beyond
factoid or tabular QA to more open-ended synthesis tasks.

E
Limitations

Evidence variable dependence. The induced evidence variable determines what evidence is recov-
erable at answer time; questions whose evidence does not factor cleanly into typed fields fall back
on free-text columns. The variables are induced from a sample of documents. If the sample misses
a kind of document, the variables can be incomplete, and evidence the question needs may not be
extracted.

Extraction completeness. The relevance gate trades a small recall loss for a large precision gain, but
rare or implicit evidence the gate underweights could be silently dropped.

Reconciliation errors. Aggressive canonicalization can collapse genuinely distinct entities, particu-
larly in domains with high-cardinality keys or near-duplicate naming conventions.

Latency. The pipeline requires multiple sequential LLM calls, resulting in higher latency (2-3 min
end-to-end) than single-call baselines; this is acceptable for accuracy-critical workflows but not for
real-time applications.

Evaluation. Evaluation relies on LLM-as-a-judge, which we validate with human annotations
(Cohen’s κ = 0.758), but residual noise remains. SLIDERS achieves 55% on FinQ100, insufficient
for full automation in high-stakes financial analysis. For safety-critical deployments, we recommend
human-in-the-loop verification: SLIDERS’s provenance tracking enables reviewers to efficiently
validate each extraction against source documents.

Provenance correctness. When provenance is incorrect (e.g., wrong text span), the reconciliation
agent may make faulty decisions; our verification of 410 facts found 99.03% correct, and users can
inspect source quotes directly.

Human in the Loop. SLIDERS is an implementation of the evidence synthesis pipeline, not a full
interactive review platform. The current system produces auditable tables and provenance, but does
not yet provide a user interface for editing variables or reconciliation decisions. Building such a
human-in-the-loop interface is future work.

Broad Societal Impact

This work has several potential positive societal impacts. Systematic reviews are central to evidence-
based decision making in medicine, social science, policy analysis, finance, law, and other technical
domains, but producing them manually requires substantial expert labor and can take many months.
By automating parts of evidence discovery, extraction, reconciliation, and synthesis, SLIDERS
could reduce the cost and time required to produce systematic-review-style analyses. This may
help researchers, analysts, policymakers, and domain experts examine larger evidence bases, ask
follow-up questions more easily, and produce syntheses with more explicit provenance than standard
retrieval-first systems. In domains such as healthcare and public policy, improved evidence synthesis
could support better-informed decisions, provided that outputs remain subject to expert review.

At the same time, the work also has potential negative societal impacts. Because SLIDERS is designed
to synthesize evidence across large document collections, errors in extraction, reconciliation, or
answer synthesis could be amplified into apparently authoritative conclusions. This risk is especially
important in high-stakes domains such as medicine, law, finance, and policy, where incorrect or
incomplete syntheses could mislead decision makers. The system may also inherit biases from the
underlying document corpus or language models, omit relevant evidence despite its goal of broad
coverage, or overstate certainty when the available evidence is incomplete or conflicting. More
broadly, automation of systematic-review-style workflows may encourage overreliance on machine-
generated syntheses, reduce incentives for careful manual validation, and advantage organizations
with access to large-scale compute and proprietary document collections. For these reasons, SLIDERS
should be used as an assistive tool rather than a replacement for expert judgment, with careful auditing
of extracted evidence, provenance, uncertainty, and failure cases.
