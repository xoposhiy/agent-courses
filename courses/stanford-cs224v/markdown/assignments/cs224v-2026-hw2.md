Part 1: Building Simple Agents

In the previous assignment, you were given a rare disease and a cohort of patients. Their
symptoms and clinical findings were encoded as Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms,
standardized labels for findings such as seizures. Out of 10 patients, exactly one patient had the
target disease. The task was to identify that patient by comparing their findings with evidence
about the disease.

In this assignment you will learn how to build agents, using a method called ReAct and then
we will learn how to use Agentic OS (AOS) for orchestrating agents.

ReAct combines reasoning and acting. The model gathers information, uses what it learns to
choose its next step, and repeats until it can answer. This approach comes from ReAct (Yao et
al., 2023)

AOS is an open, accountable platform for agents with a layered architecture that includes a
semantic file system for retrieving and curating persistent knowledge and artifacts; a harness
with experimental components to improve reasoning, context management, conversation man-
agement, and decision making; team workflow support for shared workspaces and collaboration.
Together, these components provide a common substrate for building and coordinating advanced
agent systems. AOS can provide a good foundation for your projects!

Part 2: Turning Clinical Text into Constraints

To decide whether a patient qualifies for a clinical trial, someone has to compare two documents
written in prose: the patient’s chart and the trial’s eligibility criteria. A common approach
today is to give both documents to a language model and ask it for an answer.

Part 2 studies a different approach. A language model first translates the criteria and the
chart into SMT (satisfiability modulo theories): named variables and logical constraints.
A
solver then decides eligibility from those constraints. You will see what this makes possible,
namely searching thousands of trials quickly and checking why a decision was made, and where
it falls short.

The central goal of Part 2 is to understand what the language model does, what ordinary
