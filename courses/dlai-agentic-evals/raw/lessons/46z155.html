In the last video, you evaluated the trajectory of a run: which steps happened, the order they happened in, and whether information moved correctly from one step to the next. That works well when everything you need to judge the run is contained in that one trace. But sometimes, the rule the agent breaks was established earlier in the conversation. Think about the dog shelter agent. A volunteer says, "I can only do walks before 6:00 p.m." The agent acknowledges that, and continues helping them look at dogs. A few turns later, the volunteer says, "Pepper sounds good. Can you book me for tomorrow?" The agent books Pepper for 7:00 PM. If you only looked at that last request and response, the booking might seem completely reasonable. The volunteer asked for Pepper tomorrow, Pepper was available, and the booking succeeded. The problem is that the volunteer had already said they couldn't do anything after 6:00. So the evidence you need to judge that booking isn't all in the final turn. Some of it lives earlier in the conversation. That's where the scope of the evaluation has to get wider again. Instead of looking at one trace in isolation, you look at the session, the sequence of traces that belong to the same conversation. With that, the evaluator can see both the earlier constraint and the later booking, and with that context, the failure becomes obvious. The agent understood the instruction when it was given, but it stopped respecting it later. That's one kind of multi-turn failure we usually call instruction drift. You can see something similar with Northwind. Suppose a customer says early in the conversation, "My order number is 4521." A few turns later, after discussing the item, they ask, "Can you refund it?" The relevant information was already available, yet the agent asks for the order number again, or worse, refunds a different order. In those cases, the problem is that the agent failed to carry the context forward. That's called a context loss failure. And sometimes the information does carry forward, but it doesn't propagate correctly into the later action. Imagine Northwind verifies customer C103 in one turn, Several turns later, the customer asks for a refund on order 4521. The refund action may happen in a different trace from the verification. So if your evaluator looks at only the refund trace, it might conclude that the customer was never verified. But that would be wrong. The verification did happen. It just happened earlier in the session. For a requirement like verify the customer before issuing a refund, the evaluator needs enough context to search across turns. This is why the scope of the evaluator has to match the scope of the requirement. If the question is, did the agent pass the right order ID into this tool call, one tool call may be enough. For a question like, did verification happen before the refund, one trajectory may be enough. But if verification happened several turns earlier, or if the user gave an instruction that should remain active throughout the conversation, you need to look at the entire session. Suppose you want to test one of these multi-turn behaviors repeatedly. Take this Northwind requirement: The customer does not want the refund processed until they have seen the amount and explicitly confirmed it. Here's what a correct conversation might look like. The customer starts by saying, "Don't refund anything until I confirm the amount." The agent replies, "Understood. I won't process a refund until you confirm." Then the customer asks, "How much did I pay for order 4521?" The agent looks up the order and replies, "You paid $49.99." At this point, the agent has the information it needs to issue the refund, but it still shouldn't do it. the customer hasn't confirmed yet. Then the customer says, "Okay, go ahead with the refund." Only now does the agent call process_refund for $49.99. That's the behavior you want to preserve. Now compare it with a broken version of the same conversation. The customer again says, "Don't refund anything until I confirm the amount." The agent acknowledges the instruction. Then the customer asks, "How much did I pay for order 4521?" The agent looks up the order, sees that the amount is $49.99, and immediately calls process_refund. Only after that does the customer say, "Okay, go ahead." The refund itself may be valid, the amount may be correct, and even the tool call may succeed, but the agent acted one turn too early. The failure only becomes visible when you compare what happened in the later turn with the instruction the customer gave at the beginning of the session. If you want to run this scenario against different versions of the agent, one option is to use a scripted user. You predefine the user side of the conversation, so each agent version receives the same exact messages in the same situations. For this test, both versions get, "Don't refund anything until I confirm the amount." then, "How much did I pay for order 4521?" And once the agent has reported the amount, the customer gives the go-ahead. Now the user behavior is controlled. If one version waits for that final confirmation and another processes the refund before it arrives, you have a meaningful difference in the agent's behavior, rather than a difference caused by the customer saying different things. A scripted user works especially well when you already know the interaction you want to test. However, conversations don't always follow the path you expected. Imagine another refund test where the customer begins with, "I want my money back." The agent responds, "Sure, which order do you mean?" If your test only contained a fixed sequence of pre-written messages, you now need some way to respond to that question. That's where a simulated user can be useful. Instead of specifying every exact message in advance, you assign the customer role to an LLM, and you give it a goal and the information they know. For example, the simulated customer wants a refund for Order 4521. If the agent asks, "Which order do you mean?" the simulated customer can reply, "Order 4521." If the agent then says, "The refundable amount is $49.99. Would you like me to proceed?" the simulated customer answers, "Yes." That lets the conversation adapt to what the agent actually does. The tradeoff is that the customer side can vary too. One run might phrase the answer as Order 4521, while another might say It's 4521. Or the simulated user may respond differently depending on what the agent asks. That flexibility is useful when you want to explore more natural conversations, but it also introduces another source of variation. So ultimately, the choice depends on what you're trying to evaluate. If you want two agent versions to face the exact same interaction, a scripted user gives you tighter control. If you want to test how the agent handles conversations that can branch in different directions, a simulated user gives you more flexibility. Whichever approach you use, it helps to start with a conversation you already know should pass. So go back to the Northwind refund example. In a known-good session, the agent looks up the order, tells the customer the refundable amount, waits for confirmation, and only then calls process_refund. Run your session-level evaluator on that case first. If the evaluator says this conversation failed, don't start diagnosing the agent yet. The problem may be in the evaluator or in the test setup. Once the clean case behaves as expected, run the broken session. This time, the user gives the same instruction, asks for the same amount, but the agent issues the refund before the user says "Okay, go ahead." Now the evaluator has a very specific question to answer. Did the refund happen only after the customer confirmed it? You can apply the same idea to identity verification. Suppose the conversation begins with Northwind successfully verifying customer C103. Then the customer spends several turns asking questions about order 4521. Later, they ask for a refund. If the refund happens in Turn 6, the evaluator shouldn't look only at Turn 6 and say, "I don't see any verification here." It needs to look back through the session and see that the customer was already verified in an earlier turn. That's the key idea. You don't want to give every evaluator the entire conversation just because you can. You want to give it enough evidence to answer the requirement you're checking. A wrong tool argument can be judged from just one call, while a sequence problem may need the whole trajectory. A standing instruction or a piece of context that has to persist across turns may need the whole session. Matching the evaluator scope to the requirement keeps the check focused, and makes the result easier to interpret. At this point, you can evaluate quite a lot about how the agent behaves. You can inspect individual actions, the path through a task, and requirements that stretch across several turns, but there's still one thing none of those checks can guarantee. The agent can follow the right-looking process and then tell a Northwind customer, "Your refund has been processed," or tell a dog shelter volunteer, "Your outing is booked," How do you know that the refund or booking actually exists? For that, you need to look outside the conversation and check the state of the system itself. That's what you'll do in the next video.
